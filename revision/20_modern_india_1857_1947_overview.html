Modern Indian History 1857–1947

Ninety years that turned a company's colony into two nation-states. The single densest topic in Prelims — and the one where a well-built timeline beats everything else.

How to use this page: every card is closed by default. Tap it — or hit Reveal all — only after you have tried to recall the year, the event, and the actor. Reading an open card teaches you nothing. Guessing first is what makes it stick.
How this topic is actually asked. Almost every Prelims question falls into one of four buckets:
① Year → event (which year did X happen?)
② Event → actor (who founded / led / wrote X?)
③ Sequence (which came first?)
④ Session / commission / act → outcome (which Congress session adopted Purna Swaraj?)
The card grid below is designed so any of those four queries hits one fact, not a paragraph.

1 · 1857 — the revolt and its long shadow

The event that ended Company rule and set the frame for the next ninety years.

The Great Revolt of 1857 Revolt 1857
Immediate trigger: the Enfield rifle cartridges (greased with animal fat) — offended both Hindu and Muslim sepoys at Meerut. Started: 10 May 1857, Meerut. Spread to Delhi (Bahadur Shah Zafar proclaimed leader), Kanpur (Nana Saheb), Lucknow (Begum Hazrat Mahal), Jhansi (Rani Lakshmibai), Bareilly (Khan Bahadur). Key leaders: Mangal Pandey (Barrackpore, 29 March 1857 — the first spark), Bahadur Shah Zafar (symbolic head), Tantia Tope (Jhansi–Gwalior). Result: Suppressed by 1858. Bahadur Shah Zafar exiled to Rangoon. Rani Lakshmibai died at Gwalior (June 1858). Consequence — the real exam point: The Government of India Act, 1858 abolished the East India Company and transferred power to the British Crown. The Governor-General became the Viceroy; Canning became the first Viceroy.
Queen Victoria's Proclamation British 1858
Read out by Lord Canning at Allahabad, 1 November 1858. Promised non-interference in religion, respect for treaties with princely states, and equal treatment of Indians in government service. Exam keyword: The Proclamation is the formal end of Company rule — not the Act itself. The Act transferred power; the Proclamation announced the new policy.
Indigo Revolt Peasant 1859–60
Where: Bengal — the indigo belt of Nadia, Jessore, Pabna. Against: European indigo planters who forced peasants to grow indigo on the best land at unfair prices (the tinkathia system). Outcome: The Indigo Commission (1860) was appointed; it criticised the planters. Play Nil Darpan (1860) by Dinabandhu Mitra dramatised it. This is the first great peasant movement of the modern era.
Universities of Bombay, Madras & Calcutta British 1857
Based on: Charles Wood's Despatch of 1854 — the "Magna Carta of English education in India". Why it matters: Created the English-educated middle class that would go on to found the Congress. Every nationalist of the first generation is a product of these universities.
Indian Councils Act British 1861
Key change: Introduced the portfolio system (credited to Canning) — each member of the Viceroy's Executive Council was given charge of a department. Also: Began the association of Indians with law-making — non-official Indian members could now be nominated to the Council for legislative purposes. The first step towards representative government.
Bombay–Calcutta Telegraph & other infrastructure British 1851–53
Telegraph service: Calcutta–Agra line (1853); Calcutta–Bombay (1854). By 1856 India had 4,000 miles of telegraph — which the British used to suppress the 1857 revolt. First railway: Bombay to Thane, 16 April 1853 — 34 km. The beginning of the railway age in India. Note: The telegraph and railways were built for military and commercial reasons, not Indian welfare. This is a permanent Prelims angle.
Budget system introduced British 1860
By: James Wilson — the first Finance Member of the Viceroy's Council, sent from London. Significance: India's first formal budget. Also introduced the income tax in India (1860) — a temporary measure that then became permanent.

2 · 1875–1885 — the rehearsal years before the Congress

Arya Samaj, Deccan Riots, the Census — the scaffolding of a national consciousness.

Deccan Riots Peasant 1875
Where: Poona and Ahmednagar districts (Maharashtra). Against: Marwari and Gujarati moneylenders — not the British directly. The peasants (mostly Kunbis) attacked debt bonds and moneylender property. Why it matters: Led directly to the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (1879) and helped shape the Deccan Riots Commission Report (1878). This is the archetype of the "anti-moneylender" peasant uprising in the syllabus.
Arya Samaj Reform 1875
Founder: Swami Dayanand Saraswati — Bombay, 10 April 1875. HQ later at Lahore. Programme: "Back to the Vedas"; opposed idol worship, caste by birth, and child marriage; supported Shuddhi (reconversion) and women's education; founded the D.A.V. school network. Famous work: Satyarth Prakash.
Indian Association Pre-Congress 1876
Founder: Surendranath Banerjee (with Anand Mohan Bose) — Calcutta, 1876. Significance: The most important pre-Congress political body. It organised the first all-India agitation — against the age limit for the ICS examination (reduced from 21 to 19 in 1877). Its campaign is the rehearsal for the Congress. Also known: The Indian Association was the first to demand a representative government for India.
Census system in India British 1872
First non-synchronous census: 1872, under Lord Mayo (initiated) — done in different provinces at different times. First synchronous census: 1881, under Lord Ripon — a single, coordinated count across India. Lord Ripon is therefore called the "Father of the Census in India". Exam trap: 1872 vs 1881. The answer to "first census" is 1872; the answer to "first synchronous census" is 1881.
Indian National Congress founded Congress 1885
Founded: 28 December 1885, Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay. First session presided over by W. C. Bonnerjee. Founder: A. O. Hume — a retired ICS officer. First called the "Indian National Union", renamed INC at the Bombay session. 72 delegates attended the first session. The "Safety Valve" theory (that Hume founded it to let off steam) is the most-criticised explanation — the Congress was a genuine nationalist body. Exam keyword: Bombay 1885, W. C. Bonnerjee, A. O. Hume, 72 delegates. This is a permanent Prelims quartet.

3 · 1890s–1905 — Moderates, then Extremists

From petitions to swadeshi — the split that gave the movement its teeth.

Indian Universities Commission British 1902
Chaired by: Sir Thomas Raleigh. Led to the Indian Universities Act, 1904. Effect: Tightened government control over universities — increased official nomination, put conditions on affiliation, and made the curriculum more "loyalist". Indian nationalists saw it as a clampdown on the educated class.
Partition of Bengal British 1905
Announced by: Lord Curzon — the Viceroy from 1899 to 1905. Carried out on 16 October 1905. What it did: Split Bengal into Eastern Bengal and Assam (Muslim-majority) and West Bengal (Hindu-majority). The official excuse was administrative convenience; the real aim was to divide Hindus and Muslims and weaken the nationalist movement. Reaction: The Swadeshi and Boycott Movement (1905–08) — boycott of British goods, promotion of Indian-made goods, and the beginning of the national education movement. Also gave rise to the slogan "Bande Mataram". Annulment: Partition was annulled in 1911 at the Delhi Durbar of George V — the capital was also shifted from Calcutta to Delhi in the same year.
Surat Split Congress 1907
At: Surat session of the Congress, December 1907. What happened: Open rupture between the Moderates (led by Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta) and the Extremists (led by Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal). The session broke up in chaos; the two factions held separate meetings. Key fact: The Congress was reunited at the Lucknow Session (1916) — the same session where the Lucknow Pact was signed with the Muslim League. Exam keyword: Surat 1907 = the split. Lucknow 1916 = the reunion.
Muslim League founded Communal 1906
Founded: Dacca (Dhaka), 30 December 1906 — at the annual meeting of the Muhammadan Educational Conference. Moved by: Nawab Salimullah Khan of Dacca. The name "All India Muslim League" was adopted in 1907. Context: The League's formation was encouraged by the British as a counterweight to the Congress — and the Morley–Minto Reforms (1909) then gave Muslims a separate electorate, institutionalising the divide.
Morley–Minto Reforms (Indian Councils Act, 1909) British 1909
Key change: Introduced separate electorates for Muslims — the single most consequential reform of the pre-Gandhian era. Also: Enlarged the legislative councils, allowed Indians to be associated with the executive councils, and introduced non-official members in the Imperial Legislative Council. Significance: First attempt at "representative" government — but on a communal basis. The seed of Partition is planted here.
Ghadar Party Revolutionary 1913
Founded: San Francisco, USA — by Punjabi immigrants led by Lala Har Dayal. Mouthpiece: Ghadar (in Urdu and Punjabi) — hence the name. Aim: Armed revolution to overthrow British rule in India. The Ghadar Conspiracy (1915) was crushed; the Komagata Maru incident (1914) was its immediate trigger.
Rabindranath Tagore — Nobel Prize Culture 1913
Awarded: Nobel Prize in Literature, 1913 — for Gitanjali. Significance: The first Asian to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. In the same period Tagore renounced his knighthood after the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (1919).
World War I begins World 1914
Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Empire (Turkey), Bulgaria. Allied Powers: Britain, France, Russia, Italy (joined 1915), Japan, USA (joined 1917). India's role: Over 1 million Indian soldiers served in the war. India's contribution to the British war effort became the moral basis of the Congress's demand for self-government after the war (the "Home Rule" argument).

4 · 1915–1919 — Gandhi's arrival and the post-war rupture

The turning point of the entire movement — from elite petition to mass satyagraha.

Gandhi's return to India Gandhi 9 January 1915
Arrived: Bombay, 9 January 1915 — from South Africa. That date is now Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (NRI Day). Before this: 21 years in South Africa (1893–1914). Founded the Natal Indian Congress (1894); developed satyagraha; led the Champaran-style campaigns against racial discrimination. Guru: Gopal Krishna Gokhale — Gandhi called him his political guru. He advised Gandhi to spend a year travelling India before joining politics, which Gandhi did.
Home Rule Movement Nationalist 1916
Two centres: Annie Besant (Madras — Home Rule League) and Bal Gangadhar Tilak (Poona — Indian Home Rule League). Demand: Self-government for India within the British Empire — not complete independence yet. This was the first time the demand was made at a mass level. Exam keyword: Besant's league was at Madras; Tilak's league was at Poona. The two merged informally in 1916.
Lucknow Session & Lucknow Pact Congress 1916
Two things happened at Lucknow, 1916:
  • Congress reunited — the Moderates and Extremists came back together after the 1907 Surat Split.
  • Lucknow Pact signed with the Muslim League — the Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims in exchange for League support for self-government. Jinnah, then a moderate, was the architect.
Exam trap: The Lucknow Pact is not the same as the Lucknow Session. The session = reunion; the pact = Congress–League deal.
Champaran Satyagraha Gandhi 1917
Where: Champaran district, Bihar. Against: the tinkathia system — indigo planters forcing peasants to grow indigo on 3/20 of their land. Who called Gandhi: Raj Kumar Shukla — a peasant who travelled to Lucknow to bring him. Result: The Champaran Agrarian Committee (1917) recommended abolition of the tinkathia system. It was Gandhi's first satyagraha in India — and the template for all future mass movements.
Ahmedabad Mill Strike Gandhi 1918
Where: Ahmedabad textile mills. Issue: dispute over plague bonus — the mill owners wanted to withdraw it; the workers wanted a 35% wage increase. Gandhi's method: Hunger strike (his first in India) to pressure both sides. The workers eventually got a 35% increase; Gandhi's reputation as a labour leader was cemented. Exam keyword: Ahmedabad 1918 = first hunger strike. Champaran 1917 = first satyagraha.
Kheda Satyagraha Gandhi 1918
Where: Kheda district, Gujarat. Issue: peasants unable to pay land revenue due to crop failure — demanded remission under the revenue code. Gandhi's colleagues: Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (his first major campaign) and Indulal Yagnik. Result: The government eventually suspended revenue collection — a victory. Kheda is where Vallabhbhai Patel emerged as Gandhi's lieutenant.
Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms (GoI Act, 1919) British 1919
Key feature: Introduced dyarchy in the provinces — subjects divided into transferred (run by Indian ministers) and reserved (run by the Governor). Also: Expanded the central and provincial legislative councils; introduced direct elections; separated central and provincial budgets; provided for a Central Public Service Commission (established 1926, under the Lee Commission). Why it failed: The transferred subjects were starved of funds; the Governor retained veto and special powers. Dyarchy was abolished by the GoI Act, 1935, which introduced provincial autonomy.
Rowlatt Act British 1919
Officially: the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919. What it did: Allowed detention without trial, trials without jury, and restriction of the press — for "revolutionary" offences. Gandhi called it the "Black Act". Context: Recommended by the Rowlatt Committee (1918) chaired by Justice S. A. T. Rowlatt — set up to investigate revolutionary conspiracies. The Act was passed despite unanimous opposition from every Indian member of the Imperial Legislative Council.
Jallianwala Bagh Massacre British 13 April 1919
Where: Amritsar, Punjab. By: General Reginald Dyer, on the orders of the Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab, Michael O'Dwyer. Occasion: A peaceful gathering protesting the arrest of Saifuddin Kitchlew and Satya Pal — and the Rowlatt Act. It was Baisakhi day; many in the crowd did not know about the ban on assemblies. Toll: Officially 379 killed, over 1,200 wounded. The actual figures are likely much higher. Aftermath: Tagore renounced his knighthood. The Hunter Committee (1919) was appointed to investigate — it criticised Dyer; the House of Lords voted to support him. This was a turning point that turned Gandhi from a Home Ruler into a mass leader.
Khilafat Movement Mass 1919–1924
Led by: the Ali Brothers — Muhammad Ali Jauhar and Shaukat Ali — with Maulana Abul Kalam Azad and Hakim Ajmal Khan. Issue: The fate of the Ottoman Caliph (Khalifa) after WWI; the British were dismantling the Ottoman Empire. Indian Muslims saw this as a threat to the Caliphate. Gandhi's link: Gandhi supported the Khilafat as the first great opportunity for Hindu–Muslim unity — the foundation of the Non-Cooperation Movement. Exam keyword: Khilafat 1919 + Non-Cooperation 1920 = a single combined movement. The two are almost never separated in questions.

5 · 1920–1927 — Non-Cooperation and the Swarajists

The first all-India mass movement, its abrupt end, and the constitutional debate that followed.

Non-Cooperation Movement Gandhi 1920–22
Launched: 1 August 1920 — the day Khilafat Day was observed. Approved at the Nagpur Session (December 1920) of the Congress. Programme: Surrender of titles, boycott of government schools, courts, councils, and foreign goods; promotion of swadeshi, charkha, and national education. Key slogan: "Swaraj in one year" Ended: February 1922, after the Chauri Chaura incident (5 February 1922, Gorakhpur district, UP) — a mob burnt a police station, killing 22 policemen. Gandhi withdrew the movement — a decision criticised by many leaders (Nehru, Subhas Bose). Arrested: Gandhi was arrested in March 1922 and sentenced to 6 years' imprisonment; released in 1924 on health grounds.
Chauri Chaura incident Gandhi 5 February 1922
Where: Chauri Chaura, Gorakhpur district, United Provinces (now UP). What happened: A crowd protesting police brutality clashed with police; the police fired; the crowd retaliated by setting the police station on fire, killing 22 policemen. Result: Gandhi called off the Non-Cooperation Movement — insisting that the movement must stay non-violent. This is one of the most debated decisions of his career.
Swaraj Party Congress 1923
Founded by: C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru — within the Congress, to contest the 1923 elections and enter the legislative councils. Argument: The "pro-changers" — those who wanted to enter the councils and obstruct the British from within. The "no-changers" (Rajendra Prasad, Vallabhbhai Patel, C. Rajagopalachari) opposed this. Exam keyword: Pro-changers = Swarajists = C. R. Das + Motilal Nehru. No-changers = Rajendra Prasad, Vallabhbhai Patel, Rajagopalachari.
Kakori Conspiracy / Kakori Train Action Revolutionary 9 August 1925
By: the Hindustan Republican Association (HRA) — founded by Ram Prasad Bismil, Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee, and Sachindra Nath Sanyal. What happened: A train carrying government treasury was looted near Kakori (near Lucknow) — to fund the revolutionary movement. Aftermath: Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqullah Khan, Roshan Singh, and Rajendra Lahiri were hanged. The Kakori case is the most famous trial of the revolutionary movement.
Simon Commission British 1927
Officially: the Indian Statutory Commission — set up to review the working of the GoI Act 1919, two years ahead of schedule. Why it was boycotted: All seven members were British — no Indian was included. The Congress and other parties boycotted it under the slogan "Simon Go Back". Chaired by: Sir John Simon. Members included Clement Attlee (future PM of Britain). Consequence: The boycott led to the Nehru Report (1928) — the first Indian-drafted constitution — and, in reaction, Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929). Lala Lajpat Rai: Died in November 1928 after being lathi-charged during the anti-Simon protest in Lahore. His death radicalised Bhagat Singh, who avenged it by killing J. P. Saunders.
Butler Committee British 1927
Chaired by: Sir Harcourt Butler. Set up to examine the relationship between the Indian states and the British Crown. Report: Recommended that the princely states should not be transferred to an Indian government without their consent — a safeguard for the princes. This became the basis of the paramountcy doctrine in the 1935 Act.

6 · 1928–1934 — Purna Swaraj, Dandi and the Round Tables

The decade when the demand moved from "dominion status" to "complete independence".

Nehru Report Congress 1928
Chaired by: Motilal Nehru — the report was prepared by a committee set up after the Simon boycott. Key recommendations: Dominion status for India (not complete independence); a federal structure; joint electorates (with reservation for Muslims in some provinces); fundamental rights; and a Supreme Court. Jinnah's reaction: Rejected the report and issued his Fourteen Points (1929) — demanding separate electorates, one-third representation for Muslims, and safeguards in Punjab and Bengal. Exam keyword: Nehru Report = Motilal Nehru, 1928, dominion status. Jinnah's Fourteen Points = 1929, separate electorates.
Lahore Session — Purna Swaraj Congress December 1929
Presided over by: Jawaharlal Nehru — his first Congress presidency. What was adopted: The resolution of Purna Swaraj — complete independence, not dominion status. 26 January 1930 was declared the first Independence Day. Exam keyword: Lahore 1929 = Purna Swaraj. 26 January 1930 = first Independence Day. Republic Day (26 January 1950) is chosen to honour this date.
Dandi March (Salt Satyagraha) Gandhi 12 March 1930
From: Sabarmati Ashram, Ahmedabad. To: Dandi, on the Gujarat coast — 385 km (240 miles) in 24 days. What Gandhi did: Picked up a lump of salt at Dandi on 6 April 1930 — breaking the salt law and launching the Civil Disobedience Movement. Why salt: Salt was the one item every Indian, rich or poor, used — so a tax on it hit the poorest hardest. It was also a legal monopoly of the British government. Arrest: Gandhi was arrested in May 1930 (Karachi was the planned site; he was actually arrested at Karadi, near Dandi). The movement continued, led by Abbas Tyabji and then Sarojini Naidu.
First Round Table Conference British November 1930
Held at: London. Chaired by: the British Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald. Who attended: The British government, the princely states, Muslim League, liberals, and other minorities — but not the Congress (its leaders were in jail). Outcome: Inconclusive — the British insisted on a federal structure, the princes were non-committal, and without the Congress nothing could be decided. Hence the Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931) and the Second RTC.
Gandhi–Irwin Pact Gandhi 5 March 1931
Signed between: Mahatma Gandhi and Lord Irwin — the Viceroy (1926–31). Congress agreed to: Call off the Civil Disobedience Movement and attend the Second Round Table Conference. British agreed to: Release political prisoners (except those convicted of violence), allow the manufacture of salt for personal use in coastal areas, and return confiscated property. Exam keyword: Gandhi–Irwin Pact = 5 March 1931. The British did not concede Purna Swaraj, dominion status, or any major political demand.
Second Round Table Conference British 1931
Held: London, September–December 1931. Gandhi was the sole representative of the Congress. What happened: Deadlock over the communal question — Gandhi rejected separate electorates for the depressed classes; Ambedkar insisted on them. The British refused to grant responsible government at the centre. Result: No agreement. Gandhi returned empty-handed; the Civil Disobedience Movement was relaunched (1932) and then withdrawn in 1934.
Communal Award British 16 August 1932
Announced by: Ramsay MacDonald — the British PM. Hence also called the MacDonald Award. What it did: Extended separate electorates to the depressed classes (Dalits), in addition to Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans. Gandhi's response: Began a fast unto death (20 September 1932) from Yerwada Jail — demanding the withdrawal of separate electorates for Dalits. Result: The Poona Pact (24 September 1932) — between Gandhi and Ambedkar. Separate electorates were withdrawn; reserved seats for the depressed classes in joint electorates were increased (from 71 to 147 in provincial legislatures).
Poona Pact Gandhi 24 September 1932
Between: Gandhi (on behalf of caste Hindus) and Dr. B. R. Ambedkar (on behalf of the depressed classes). Agreed: Joint electorates with reserved seats for the depressed classes — abandoning separate electorates. Significance: The most important Gandhi–Ambedkar negotiation. Gandhi ended his fast the same day. It is the founding text of Dalit political representation in India.
Government of India Act, 1935 British 1935
The longest Act ever passed by the British Parliament — 451 clauses, 15 schedules. Key features:
  • Provincial Autonomy — replaced dyarchy in provinces with responsible government (elected ministers in charge of all provincial subjects).
  • Dyarchy at the Centre — introduced (though it never actually worked).
  • All-India Federation — proposed, but never came into being (the princely states refused to join).
  • Separate electorates extended — to depressed classes, women, and labour.
  • Federal Court established (1937) — the precursor of the Supreme Court.
  • RBI — already created in 1935, given more statutory powers.
  • Public Service Commission at the federal level (Federal Public Service Commission).
Congress and the Act: Opposed it — but contested the 1937 elections under it, and formed ministries in 8 out of 11 provinces.

7 · 1935–1942 — Ministries, war, and the last mass movement

The road from provincial power to Quit India.

All India Kisan Congress Congress 1936
Founded: Lucknow, April 1936. Also called the All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS). Founders: Swami Sahajanand Saraswati (first president), with N. G. Ranga, E. M. S. Namboodiripad, and others. Aim: Abolition of zamindari, reduction of rent and revenue, and protection of peasants' rights. This was the first major peasant organisation at an all-India level.
Congress ministries resign Congress October–November 1939
Why: The Viceroy (Linlithgow) declared India a belligerent in World War II without consulting Indian leaders. The Congress demanded a clear commitment to Indian self-government after the war; the British refused. Result: Congress ministries resigned in all 8 provinces between October and November 1939. The Muslim League observed it as a "Day of Deliverance" (22 December 1939). Exam keyword: "Day of Deliverance" = Muslim League, December 1939, celebrating the resignation of Congress ministries.
August Offer British 8 August 1940
By: Lord Linlithgow — the Viceroy. A response to the Congress's demand for a clear post-war constitutional settlement. Key promises: Dominion status as the objective; expansion of the Viceroy's Executive Council with more Indians; a constituent assembly to frame the constitution after the war; and a guarantee of minority rights. Reaction: Congress rejected it — no immediate transfer of power, and the "minority veto" clause was unacceptable. Jinnah also rejected it, as it did not concede Pakistan. The Individual Satyagraha (1940) followed.
Individual Satyagraha Gandhi 1940–41
Launched by: Gandhi — because the Congress was not ready for a mass movement, but the leadership wanted to signal opposition to the war effort. Method: Selected individuals (not masses) would offer satyagraha by making anti-war speeches — and be arrested. First satyagrahi: Acharya Vinoba Bhave. Second: Jawaharlal Nehru. Over 25,000 satyagrahis were arrested. Exam keyword: Vinoba Bhave = first individual satyagrahi (1940). A permanent one-line answer.
Atlantic Charter World August 1941
Between: US President Franklin D. Roosevelt and British PM Winston Churchill — on board a warship in the Atlantic. Key principles: No territorial aggrandisement; self-determination of peoples; free trade; freedom from fear and want. India's angle: Churchill insisted the Charter applied only to Europe — not to the British Empire. Gandhi and Nehru pointed out the double standard. It became an argument for Indian self-determination.
Cripps Mission British March 1942
By: Sir Stafford Cripps — a Labour Party leader, sent by Churchill's government. Offered: Dominion status after the war; a constituent assembly elected by the provincial legislatures and princely states; and an option for provinces to stay out of the Union (the seed of Pakistan). Rejected by: Congress (no immediate transfer of power, no real defence portfolio) and the Muslim League (no clear Pakistan). Consequence: Its failure triggered the Quit India Movement — Gandhi's "Do or Die" speech.
Quit India Movement Mass 8 August 1942
Launched: 8 August 1942 — at the Gowalia Tank Maidan (August Kranti Maidan), Bombay. Approved by the All India Congress Committee. Gandhi's slogan: "Do or Die" — and "Karo ya Maro". Demand: Immediate end of British rule in India. Arrests: Within hours, the entire Congress leadership was arrested — Gandhi (at Aga Khan Palace, Poona), Nehru, Patel, Azad, and others. Gandhi was released in 1944 on health grounds. Who led the underground: Jayaprakash Narayan (JP), Ram Manohar Lohia, Aruna Asaf Ali (hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank), Usha Mehta (ran the underground Congress Radio), and Sucheta Kripalani. Exam keyword: Quit India = 8 August 1942, Gowalia Tank, "Do or Die". Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the flag; Usha Mehta ran Congress Radio.
Indian National Army (INA) World 1942–45
Founded: Initially 1942 by Mohan Singh (with Japanese help) in Southeast Asia, after the fall of Singapore. Reorganised: By Subhas Chandra Bose — who arrived in Singapore in July 1943 and took over the INA, also forming the Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind) in October 1943. Bose's famous line: "Give me blood, and I will give you freedom." Women's wing: The Rani Jhansi Regiment — commanded by Captain Lakshmi Sahgal (Lakshmi Swaminathan). Last stand: The INA fought alongside the Japanese in Imphal and Kohima (1944) and retreated with the Japanese. Bose died in a plane crash in Taiwan (18 August 1945) — the exact circumstances are still disputed. Legacy: The INA trials at the Red Fort (1945–46) triggered riots and turned the Indian public — and even the British Indian Army — against the Raj. It is the single biggest reason the British lost the loyalty of the Indian armed forces.

8 · 1944–1947 — The endgame

Six years, five plans, three missions, one Radcliffe Line.

Shimla Conference British 1945
Called by: Lord Wavell — the Viceroy. Held at Shimla, June–July 1945. Aim: To agree on the composition of the Executive Council — to give Indians a majority in the Viceroy's council. Failed because: Jinnah insisted the League had the right to nominate all Muslim members, which the Congress rejected. Wavell's "breakdown plan" followed.
Wavell Plan British June 1945
Key proposal: Reconstituted Executive Council with all members Indians except the Viceroy and the Commander-in-Chief. An equal number of "caste Hindus" and Muslims, plus one Sikh, one from the depressed classes. Congress demand: It should have the right to nominate one Muslim member, and should be recognised as a national body representing all communities. League demand: The League alone should nominate all Muslim members. Result: Deadlock. The Shimla Conference failed.
Cabinet Mission British 1946
Members: Pethick-Lawrence (Secretary of State for India, Chairman), Stafford Cripps, and A. V. Alexander — three British cabinet ministers. Proposal:
  • A union of India with a weak centre (defence, foreign affairs, communications only)
  • Grouping of provinces into three sections (A, B, C) — with the option to secede from the union after 10 years
  • A constituent assembly elected by the provincial legislatures
  • Interim government with Congress and League participation
Reaction: Both the Congress and the League accepted the plan initially, with different interpretations. The Congress opposed compulsory grouping; the League wanted it, because it would enable Pakistan. Result: The plan collapsed over the grouping issue. Jinnah called for "Direct Action Day" (16 August 1946), which led to the Great Calcutta Killings.
Direct Action Day / Great Calcutta Killings Mass 16 August 1946
Called by: the Muslim League — Jinnah's response to the Congress's rejection of compulsory grouping under the Cabinet Mission plan. Result: Rioting in Calcutta over four days — estimated 4,000–10,000 dead. Communal violence spread to Noakhali, Bihar, and the Punjab. Gandhi walked barefoot through Noakhali in 1946–47 to restore peace. Exam keyword: Direct Action Day = 16 August 1946 = Great Calcutta Killings. This is the point of no return for Partition.
Interim Government Congress 2 September 1946
Formed: 2 September 1946 — with Jawaharlal Nehru as Vice-President of the Executive Council (effectively Prime Minister). Composition: Congress held the key portfolios (Home, External Affairs, Finance, Defence, etc.); the Muslim League joined in October 1946 but effectively boycotted it from 1947. Why it matters: This was the first Indian government — the transfer of power had effectively begun even before the formal date.
Constituent Assembly formed Constitution 9 December 1946
First met: 9 December 1946, in the Constitution Hall, New Delhi. Sachchidananda Sinha was appointed interim chairman. On 11 December 1946: Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected permanent President. H. C. Mukherjee was elected Vice-President. B. N. Rau was appointed constitutional adviser. Objective Resolution: Moved by Jawaharlal Nehru on 13 December 1946 — the philosophical foundation of the Constitution. Adopted 22 January 1947. Drafting Committee: Chaired by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar — with 7 members including Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar, N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, K. M. Munshi, T. T. Krishnamachari, and S. N. Mukherjee. Adopted: 26 November 1949. Came into force: 26 January 1950 (Republic Day).
Mountbatten Plan British 3 June 1947
By: Lord Mountbatten — the last Viceroy (March 1947 – August 1947). Key provisions:
  • Partition of India into two dominions — India and Pakistan
  • Provincial assemblies to decide whether to join India, Pakistan, or stay independent (in the case of Bengal and Punjab, the decision would be by separate votes of Hindu and Muslim members)
  • A referendum in the NWFP and Sylhet (Assam)
  • A Boundary Commission under Sir Cyril Radcliffe
  • Princely states free to join either dominion or remain independent
Accepted by: Congress (reluctantly), Muslim League (after initial hesitation), and the Sikhs (reluctantly). Date set: 15 August 1947 — a year earlier than the British had planned (June 1948), because Mountbatten feared the collapse of order.
Indian Independence Act British 18 July 1947
Passed by: the British Parliament, 18 July 1947. Based on the Mountbatten Plan. Provision: Created two independent dominions — India and Pakistan — from 15 August 1947, with the right to secede from the Commonwealth. Abolished the office of the Secretary of State for India. The British suzerainty over the princely states lapsed. Note: The Act received royal assent on 18 July 1947. It is the legal instrument of Indian independence — not a mere declaration.
Radcliffe Line Partition 17 August 1947
Drawn by: Sir Cyril Radcliffe — a British lawyer who had never been to India before. What it did: Divided Punjab and Bengal between India and Pakistan. The line was drawn in just five weeks, using outdated maps and census data. Published: 17 August 1947 — two days after independence, so the actual line was not known on 15 August. Consequence: The largest migration in human history — roughly 14–15 million people displaced. Estimates of deaths range from 200,000 to 2 million.
Royal Indian Navy Mutiny Mutiny 18 February 1946
Started: Bombay, on HMIS Talwar — a ratings' (sailors') mutiny over working conditions and food, and against the treatment of INA prisoners. Spread to: Karachi, Calcutta, Madras, and other ports. Around 20,000 sailors participated; the flag of the Provisional Government of Free India was hoisted in some places. Suppressed by: 23 February 1946 — with the personal intervention of Vallabhbhai Patel and Jinnah, who asked the ratings to surrender. Exam keyword: RIN Mutiny = February 1946 — the naval counterpart of the INA trials. It showed the British that even the Indian armed forces were no longer reliable. This is the last major pre-independence event before the Cabinet Mission.
"Jai Hind" — the phrase Culture 1940s
Popularised by: Subhas Chandra Bose and the Indian National Army. The INA used it as its greeting. Originally: Coined by Major Abid Hasan Safrani — a Hyderabadi INA officer — as a shortened form of "Jai Hindustan". Bose adopted it as the INA's official salutation. After independence: "Jai Hind" became a standard Indian greeting, still used by the armed forces and in public life.

9 · Memory tricks that save you

The four Rs of 1857–58. "Revolt → Repression → Royal Proclamation → Rule of Crown"
1857 Revolt → 1858 suppression and exile of Bahadur Shah Zafar → Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 Nov 1858) → Government of India Act 1858 transfers power from Company to Crown. Four steps, one year, one chain.
The Congress presidencies you must never mix. "First Bombay Bonnerjee, First Muslim Badruddin, First Woman Sarojini, First Nehru Motilal 1919"
1885 Bombay: W. C. Bonnerjee (first session). 1887 Madras: Badruddin Tyabji (first Muslim president). 1917 Calcutta: Annie Besant (first woman president). 1919 Amritsar: Motilal Nehru (first of the Nehru family). 1929 Lahore: Jawaharlal Nehru (youngest till then; Purna Swaraj). Get these five, and Prelims can't trap you.
Gandhi's five early satyagrahas — by year, by issue. "Champaran Indigo 17, Ahmedabad Strike 18, Kheda Revenue 18"
Champaran (1917) — indigo planters, Bihar. Ahmedabad (1918) — mill workers, wage dispute, first hunger strike. Kheda (1918) — peasants, revenue remission, Vallabhbhai Patel's debut. Three campaigns, three issues — never swap them.
The three Round Table Conferences. "First no Congress, Second only Gandhi, Third no Gandhi"
1st RTC (Nov 1930): Congress boycotted — its leaders in jail. 2nd RTC (Sep 1931): Gandhi the sole Congress delegate, deadlock over communal question. 3rd RTC (Nov 1932): Gandhi back in India, Congress not represented. The Congress did not attend the 3rd RTC.
Gandhi vs Ambedkar — one negotiation, two vocabularies. "Communal Award → Gandhi fast → Poona Pact"
The British award separate electorates for the depressed classes (16 Aug 1932) → Gandhi begins a fast unto death from Yerwada Jail (20 Sep 1932) → Gandhi and Ambedkar sign the Poona Pact (24 Sep 1932), agreeing to joint electorates with reserved seats. This is the single most important Gandhi–Ambedkar negotiation — remember the sequence.
The last three British plans — in order. "Cripps, Wavell, Cabinet"
Cripps Mission (March 1942): Dominion status after the war; rejected by both Congress and League; triggered Quit India. Wavell Plan (June 1945): All-Indian Executive Council; failed over Muslim nomination. Cabinet Mission (1946): Three-tier federal union with groupings; collapsed over the grouping issue. Three plans, three failures, one road to Partition.
The last Viceroy and the last Governor-General. "Mountbatten the last Viceroy, Rajagopalachari the last Governor-General"
Lord Mountbatten = last Viceroy (March–August 1947) AND the first Governor-General of independent India (1947–48). C. Rajagopalachari = the last Governor-General (1948–1950) and the first Indian to hold the post. If the question asks "who was the last Governor-General", the answer is Rajaji — not Mountbatten.
The three dates that always get confused. "26 Nov 49 adopted, 26 Jan 50 came into force, 26 Jan 30 first Independence Day"
26 November 1949: Constitution adopted by the Constituent Assembly. 26 January 1950: Constitution came into force — Republic Day. 26 January 1930: First Independence Day — declared at the Lahore Session (1929). Three dates, three different meanings. Never mix them.
Watch these four traps. ① "First census in India" = 1872 (non-synchronous). "First synchronous census" = 1881. ② The Lucknow Session (1916) = Congress reunion; the Lucknow Pact (1916) = Congress–League deal. Different things. ③ The Simon Commission was all-British — that is why it was boycotted. Not because of its recommendations. ④ The Government of India Act 1919 introduced dyarchy in the provinces; the Government of India Act 1935 introduced dyarchy at the centre and provincial autonomy. The two are the same phrase for two different things — read the year carefully.

10 · Rapid Fire

Cover the whole page. Answer out loud before you tap.

1. When did the revolt of 1857 begin, where, and what was the immediate trigger?

10 May 1857, Meerut. The immediate trigger was the Enfield rifle cartridge greased with animal fat, which offended both Hindu and Muslim sepoys. Mangal Pandey had already sparked resistance at Barrackpore on 29 March 1857.

2. Which Act abolished the East India Company and when?

The Government of India Act, 1858 — it transferred power from the Company to the British Crown. The Governor-General became the Viceroy; Lord Canning became the first Viceroy.

3. What was the Indigo Revolt, where did it happen, and which commission followed it?

1859–60, Bengal — against European indigo planters and the tinkathia system. The Indigo Commission (1860) was appointed, which criticised the planters. The play Nil Darpan (1860) by Dinabandhu Mitra dramatised it.

4. Which event is called the first all-India agitation of the pre-Congress era, and who led it?

The campaign against the reduction of the ICS examination age limit (from 21 to 19) in 1877 — led by Surendranath Banerjee and the Indian Association.

5. Where was the Indian National Congress founded, in which year, and who was its first president?

Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay, 28 December 1885. First president: W. C. Bonnerjee. Founder: A. O. Hume. 72 delegates attended the first session.

6. What was the Deccan Riots, when did it happen, and who was the target?

1875, Poona and Ahmednagar districts (Maharashtra). The target was Marwari and Gujarati moneylenders — not the British. It led to the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act (1879).

7. What was the first synchronous census in India and under which Viceroy?

1881, under Lord Ripon. The first non-synchronous census was 1872, under Lord Mayo.

8. When was the Partition of Bengal announced, by whom, and when was it annulled?

Announced by Lord Curzon; carried out 16 October 1905. Annulled in 1911 at the Delhi Durbar of George V — when the capital was also shifted from Calcutta to Delhi.

9. What happened at the Surat Session of 1907 and when were the two factions reunited?

The Surat Split — a rupture between Moderates (Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta) and Extremists (Tilak, Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal). Reunited at the Lucknow Session, 1916.

10. When was the Muslim League founded, where, and by whom?

30 December 1906, Dacca (Dhaka), at the Muhammadan Educational Conference. Moved by Nawab Salimullah Khan. The name "All India Muslim League" was adopted in 1907.

11. What was the key feature of the Morley–Minto Reforms (1909)?

Introduction of separate electorates for Muslims — the single most consequential reform of the pre-Gandhian era. Also enlarged legislative councils and allowed Indians into executive councils.

12. When did Gandhi return to India, and which Congress leader did he regard as his political guru?

9 January 1915, Bombay — now celebrated as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas. His political guru was Gopal Krishna Gokhale.

13. Who founded the two Home Rule Leagues, and where was each based?

Annie Besant — Home Rule League at Madras. Bal Gangadhar Tilak — Indian Home Rule League at Poona. Both founded 1916.

14. What happened at the Lucknow Session of 1916?

Two things: the Congress reunited (Moderates and Extremists after the 1907 Surat Split), and the Lucknow Pact was signed with the Muslim League — the Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims.

15. What was Gandhi's first satyagraha in India, when, where, and against what?

Champaran Satyagraha, 1917, Bihar — against the tinkathia system of indigo planters. Raj Kumar Shukla brought Gandhi to Champaran. The Champaran Agrarian Committee (1917) recommended abolition of tinkathia.

16. What was Gandhi's first hunger strike in India, and on what issue?

The Ahmedabad Mill Strike, 1918 — a dispute over the plague bonus; workers demanded a 35% wage increase. Gandhi's hunger strike pressured both sides; the workers got 35%.

17. Who were Gandhi's key lieutenants in the Kheda Satyagraha?

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (his first major campaign, along with Indulal Yagnik). Kheda, 1918 — peasants unable to pay land revenue after crop failure.

18. What did the Rowlatt Act do, and why was it called the "Black Act"?

Officially the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919. It allowed detention without trial, trials without jury, and press restrictions. Gandhi called it the "Black Act". It was passed despite unanimous Indian opposition in the Imperial Legislative Council.

19. What happened at Jallianwala Bagh, when, and who was responsible?

13 April 1919, Amritsar — General Reginald Dyer ordered firing on a peaceful gathering protesting the Rowlatt Act and the arrest of Saifuddin Kitchlew and Satya Pal. Officially 379 killed; likely more. The Hunter Committee (1919) investigated; the House of Lords voted to support Dyer.

20. What was the Khilafat Movement, who led it, and why did Gandhi support it?

1919–1924 — a movement to protect the Ottoman Caliph after WWI. Led by the Ali Brothers (Muhammad Ali Jauhar, Shaukat Ali), Maulana Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan. Gandhi supported it as the first great opportunity for Hindu–Muslim unity — and it became the foundation of the Non-Cooperation Movement.

21. When was the Non-Cooperation Movement launched and when was it withdrawn?

Launched 1 August 1920; approved at the Nagpur Session (December 1920). Withdrawn in February 1922 after the Chauri Chaura incident (5 February 1922, Gorakhpur district, UP) — a mob burnt a police station, killing 22 policemen.

22. What was the Swaraj Party, who founded it, and what was the "pro-changer / no-changer" debate?

Founded 1923 by C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru. Pro-changers (Swarajists) wanted to enter the councils and obstruct the British from within. No-changers (Rajendra Prasad, Vallabhbhai Patel, C. Rajagopalachari) opposed council entry.

23. What was the Kakori Conspiracy, when, and who was hanged?

9 August 1925 — the Hindustan Republican Association (founded by Ram Prasad Bismil) looted a train near Kakori carrying government treasury. Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqullah Khan, Roshan Singh and Rajendra Lahiri were hanged.

24. What was the Simon Commission, why was it boycotted, and who chaired it?

The Indian Statutory Commission, 1927 — to review the GoI Act 1919. Chaired by Sir John Simon. Boycotted because all seven members were British — no Indian was included. Slogan: "Simon Go Back". Lala Lajpat Rai died after being lathi-charged during the Lahore protest (Nov 1928).

25. What was the Nehru Report, who chaired it, and what did Jinnah issue in response?

Nehru Report, 1928 — chaired by Motilal Nehru; recommended dominion status, joint electorates, fundamental rights. Jinnah rejected it and issued his Fourteen Points (1929) demanding separate electorates and one-third Muslim representation.

26. When and where was Purna Swaraj adopted, and who presided over the session?

Lahore Session, December 1929 — presided over by Jawaharlal Nehru. 26 January 1930 was declared the first Independence Day.

27. What was the Dandi March, when did it begin, and what did it launch?

Began 12 March 1930 from Sabarmati Ashram — 385 km to Dandi, Gujarat. Gandhi picked up salt at Dandi on 6 April 1930, breaking the salt law and launching the Civil Disobedience Movement.

28. What was the Gandhi–Irwin Pact, when was it signed, and what did each side concede?

5 March 1931, between Gandhi and Lord Irwin. Congress agreed to call off Civil Disobedience and attend the 2nd RTC. British agreed to release political prisoners (except those convicted of violence), allow personal salt-making, and return confiscated property.

29. What was the Communal Award, who announced it, and what was the Poona Pact?

Communal Award — 16 August 1932, by Ramsay MacDonald; extended separate electorates to the depressed classes. Gandhi began a fast unto death (20 Sep 1932). The Poona Pact (24 Sep 1932) between Gandhi and Ambedkar withdrew separate electorates for Dalits and increased reserved seats in joint electorates from 71 to 147.

30. When was the Government of India Act 1935 passed, and what was its most important feature?

1935 — the longest Act ever passed by the British Parliament. Introduced provincial autonomy (replacing dyarchy in provinces), dyarchy at the centre, an All-India Federation (never implemented), a Federal Court, and extended separate electorates. Congress opposed it but contested the 1937 elections under it and formed ministries in 8 of 11 provinces.

31. What was the August Offer, when was it made, and by whom?

8 August 1940, by Lord Linlithgow. Promised dominion status as the objective, expansion of the Executive Council with more Indians, a constituent assembly after the war, and minority safeguards. Rejected by both Congress and the Muslim League.

32. Who was the first individual satyagrahi in 1940, and who was the second?

Acharya Vinoba Bhave was the first; Jawaharlal Nehru was the second. Over 25,000 satyagrahis were arrested.

33. What was the Cripps Mission, when did it come, and what did it offer?

March 1942, led by Sir Stafford Cripps. Offered dominion status after the war, a constituent assembly elected by provincial legislatures and princely states, and an option for provinces to stay out of the Union. Rejected by both Congress and the Muslim League — its failure triggered Quit India.

34. When and where was the Quit India Movement launched, and what was Gandhi's slogan?

8 August 1942, Gowalia Tank Maidan (August Kranti Maidan), Bombay. Gandhi's slogan was "Do or Die" (Karo ya Maro). The entire Congress leadership was arrested within hours.

35. Who hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank during Quit India, and who ran the underground Congress Radio?

Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the flag. Usha Mehta ran the underground Congress Radio. The underground was led by Jayaprakash Narayan, Ram Manohar Lohia, and Sucheta Kripalani.

36. Who founded the INA, who reorganised it, and who commanded the women's wing?

Founded in 1942 by Mohan Singh with Japanese help. Reorganised by Subhas Chandra Bose in 1943. The Rani Jhansi Regiment (women's wing) was commanded by Captain Lakshmi Sahgal.

37. What was the Cabinet Mission, who were its members, and what did it propose?

1946 — three British cabinet ministers: Pethick-Lawrence (Chairman), Stafford Cripps, and A. V. Alexander. Proposed a union of India with a weak centre, grouping of provinces into three sections (A, B, C), a constituent assembly, and an interim government. Collapsed over the grouping issue.

38. What was Direct Action Day, when did it happen, and what was its consequence?

16 August 1946, called by the Muslim League after the Congress rejected compulsory grouping under the Cabinet Mission plan. Led to the Great Calcutta Killings — 4,000–10,000 dead. Communal violence spread to Noakhali, Bihar, and Punjab.

39. When was the Interim Government formed, and who was its de facto head?

2 September 1946 — Jawaharlal Nehru as Vice-President of the Executive Council (effectively Prime Minister). The Muslim League joined in October 1946 but boycotted from 1947.

40. When did the Constituent Assembly first meet, who was interim chairman, and who was elected permanent President?

First met 9 December 1946; Sachchidananda Sinha was interim chairman. On 11 December 1946, Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected permanent President; H. C. Mukherjee was elected Vice-President; B. N. Rau was appointed constitutional adviser.

41. Who moved the Objectives Resolution, when, and what was its significance?

Jawaharlal Nehru, 13 December 1946. The philosophical foundation of the Indian Constitution — adopted 22 January 1947.

42. Who chaired the Drafting Committee of the Constitution, and when was the Constitution adopted and enforced?

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee. Adopted 26 November 1949. Came into force 26 January 1950 (Republic Day).

43. What was the Mountbatten Plan, when was it announced, and what date did it set for independence?

3 June 1947 — partition of India into two dominions, provincial assemblies to decide their future, a referendum in NWFP and Sylhet, a Boundary Commission under Radcliffe. Set 15 August 1947 — a year earlier than the British had planned (June 1948).

44. What did the Indian Independence Act 1947 do, and when did it receive royal assent?

Passed 18 July 1947 — created two independent dominions (India and Pakistan) from 15 August 1947, with the right to secede from the Commonwealth. Abolished the office of the Secretary of State for India and ended British suzerainty over the princely states.

45. Who drew the Radcliffe Line, when was it published, and what was its consequence?

Sir Cyril Radcliffe — a British lawyer who had never been to India. The line was published 17 August 1947, two days after independence. It displaced 14–15 million people; deaths are estimated between 200,000 and 2 million.

46. What was the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny, when did it happen, and who asked the ratings to surrender?

18 February 1946, starting at Bombay on HMIS Talwar — over working conditions, food, and the treatment of INA prisoners. Spread to Karachi, Calcutta, Madras; ~20,000 sailors participated. Suppressed by 23 February 1946, after Vallabhbhai Patel and Jinnah asked the ratings to surrender.

47. Who was the last Viceroy of India, and who was the last Governor-General?

Lord Mountbatten was the last Viceroy (March–August 1947) and the first Governor-General of independent India (1947–48). C. Rajagopalachari was the last Governor-General (1948–1950) and the first Indian to hold the post.

48. Who coined "Jai Hind", and who popularised it?

Coined by Major Abid Hasan Safrani — a Hyderabadi INA officer — as a shortened form of "Jai Hindustan". Popularised by Subhas Chandra Bose and the INA as its official salutation.
