Foreign Travellers to India

c. 300 BCE → 1900 CE. The outside eye on India's polity, economy, society and religion — and the single most reliable source of Prelims one-liners in Ancient & Medieval India.

How to use this page: every card is closed by default. Tap it — or hit Reveal all — only after you have tried to recall the traveller, the ruler, and the book. Reading an open card teaches you nothing. Guessing first is what makes it stick.
The four ways this topic is asked. Almost every Prelims question on foreign travellers falls into one of these:
① Match the traveller to the ruler (Ibn Battuta ↔ Muhammad bin Tughlaq)
② Match the traveller to the book (Al-Biruni ↔ Kitab-ul-Hind)
③ Match the traveller to the nationality (Abdur Razzaq ↔ Persia)
④ Chronological order (who came first — Conti or Paes?)
Build the card grid in your head so that any of the four queries hits a single fact.

1 · The ancients — Greeks and the Chinese trio (c. 300 BCE – 700 CE)

Not in the PDF tables, but in the timeline image — and asked far more often than you'd expect.

Megasthenes Greece c. 300 BCE
Ruler / court: Chandragupta Maurya Book: Indica Who he was: Ambassador of Seleucus Nicator at the Mauryan court — the first Greek eyewitness to India. Key observations:
  • Divided Indian society into seven classes (philosophers, farmers, herdsmen, artisans, soldiers, overseers, councillors)
  • Claimed there was no slavery in India — widely disputed by modern historians
  • Described a large standing army, a well-organised bureaucracy, and a land-revenue system
Exam keyword: The single most-quoted foreign source for Mauryan administration — even though much of it is hearsay.
Faxian (Fa-Hien) China 399–414 CE
Ruler: Chandragupta II Vikramaditya (Gupta) Book: Record of Buddhist Kingdoms (Foguo Ji) Route: Came overland via Central Asia; returned by sea through Sri Lanka and Java. Key observations:
  • Gupta administration was mild and liberal — no capital punishment, no harsh penal code
  • Described flourishing Buddhist monasteries and the prosperity of the Gangetic plain
  • Noted the Chandals (untouchables) lived outside the city, and that vegetarianism was widespread
Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsang) China c. 629–645 CE
Ruler: Harshavardhana Book: Si-Yu-Ki (Records of the Western Regions) Key facts:
  • Studied at Nalanda for 5 years under Acharya Shilabhadra
  • Carried 657 Buddhist texts back to China — the largest single transfer of Sanskrit manuscripts
  • Described Harsha's court, the Prayag Assembly (Kumbha Mela), Harsha's land grants to Nalanda, and the decline of Buddhism in parts of India
  • His account is the principal source for Harsha's reign — the Prayag assembly and Kanauj assembly are known mainly through him
Exam keyword: "Who described Harsha's assembly at Prayag?" — Xuanzang.
Yijing (I-Tsing) China c. 671–695 CE
Route: Sailed from China to India by sea — unlike Xuanzang, who came overland. Key facts:
  • Spent 10 years at Nalanda (twice as long as Xuanzang)
  • Translated Sanskrit works into Chinese and vice versa
  • Book: A Record of the Buddhist Religion as Practised in India and the Malay Archipelago
  • His account describes Nalanda's administration, the rules of monastic life, and the state of Buddhism in the late 7th century
Exam keyword: Xuanzang studied 5 years at Nalanda; Yijing stayed 10. Never mix them.

2 · Early medieval — Arab merchants and the Central Asian scholar (851–1025 CE)

The Arab geographers tell us about the Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas. Al-Biruni tells us about almost everything else.

Sulaiman Arab c. 851 CE
Rulers contemporary to him: Mihira Bhoja (Gurjara-Pratihara), Amoghavarsha (Rashtrakuta), Pala rulers in Bengal Book: Kitab al-Masalik wa al-Mamalik (Book of Roads and Kingdoms) Key observations:
  • An Arab merchant who described the political and commercial conditions of 9th-century India
  • Called the Bengal region "Ruhma" (the Pala kingdom) and Rashtrakuta territory "Ruhimi"
  • Named the Gurjara-Pratihara king as "Ballhara" — the most powerful sovereign of the time, a title Arab writers used for him
Exam keyword: "Ballhara" = the Pratihara king. A classic one-word answer.
Al-Biruni Uzbekistan 1017–1025 CE
Ruler / patron: Mahmud of Ghazni Book: Tahqiq-i-Hind (also called Kitab-ul-Hind) Method — the part UPSC tests:
  • Learned Sanskrit and studied the Vedas, Puranas, astronomy, mathematics, and geography
  • Used a scientific, comparative method — highly objective, not a moralising travelogue
  • Explained Indian time-calculation, astronomy, and the caste system
His two famous observations:
  • Hindus excel in science but are socially conservative
  • Criticised the Brahmin reluctance to share knowledge with outsiders and lower castes
Exam keyword: Al-Biruni's Kitab-ul-Hind is the first genuinely scientific foreign account of India — not a merchant's logbook, not a pilgrim's diary.

3 · Sultanate era — the two that matter most (1292–1347 CE)

Marco Polo sees the South. Ibn Battuta sees the court of the most controversial Sultan of Delhi.

Marco Polo Italy 1292–1294 CE
Region / rulers: Pandya kingdom and Kakatiya territory (South India) Book: The Travels of Marco Polo (Il Milione) Key observations:
  • Visited the Coromandel Coast (Pandya region) — not North India
  • Described pearl fisheries, the climate, and coastal social life
  • Also described the diamond mines of the Deccan and the prosperity of the South Indian ports
Exam trap: Marco Polo is often wrongly tied to the Delhi Sultanate. He only saw the South — he was on his way home from China.
Ibn Battuta Morocco 1333–1347 CE
Ruler: Muhammad bin Tughlaq Book: Rihla ("The Travels") The single most-asked fact: He was appointed Qazi of Delhi by Muhammad bin Tughlaq — a foreigner given a senior judicial post. His observations on the Sultan:
  • Described Muhammad bin Tughlaq as capable but unpredictable — a ruler whose projects outran his means
  • Noted the harsh punishments, the token currency experiment, and the efficient postal system (both horse-post and foot-post)
Places he travelled: Delhi → Malabar Coast → Maldives → Ceylon Also recorded: the prevalence of slavery in India — a sobering contrast to Megasthenes' claim 1,600 years earlier.

4 · Vijayanagara & the Portuguese century (1419–1535 CE)

Six travellers, one empire. This is the highest-density cluster in the whole topic.

Niccolò Conti Italy 1419–1439 CE
Rulers: Deva Raya I and Deva Raya II (Vijayanagara) Key facts:
  • Travelled disguised as a Muslim merchant — an Italian in Arab dress to move safely through the Deccan
  • Visited Vijayanagara in the early 15th century, BEFORE the Portuguese arrived
  • Provided a rare early European description of the city — its walls, markets, and the grandeur of the court
Exam keyword: "Which European visited Vijayanagara before the Portuguese?" — Niccolò Conti.
Abdur Razzaq Persia 1442–1445 CE
Rulers: Deva Raya II (Vijayanagara) and the Zamorin (Calicut) Book: Matla-us-Sadain wa Majma-ul-Bahrain Key facts:
  • First halted at Calicut under the Zamorin, then travelled to Vijayanagara
  • Sent as an envoy by Shah Rukh Mirza of Persia — a diplomatic mission, not a trade one
  • Described massive wealth, a large army, and elaborate court ritual
  • Gave a detailed account of the royal procession and urban grandeur — his description of the Mahanavami Dibba festival is a primary source
Afanasy Nikitin Russia 1469–1472 CE
Ruler / region: Bahmani Sultanate (Deccan) Book: Voyage Beyond Three Seas (Khozheniye za tri morya) Key fact: One of the earliest Russian accounts of India — a Tver merchant who reached the Deccan via the Caspian, Persian Gulf and Arabian Sea. His account is almost entirely about the Bahmani kingdom, not Vijayanagara.
Duarte Barbosa Portugal 1500–1516 CE
Ruler: Vira Narasimha (Vijayanagara) Book: Book of Duarte Barbosa Key observations:
  • Described coastal trade networks and society — the Malabar and Kanara ports in detail
  • Explicitly mentions diamond mines in the Vijayanagara region — the earliest European reference to them
Note: He was a Portuguese official in India, and Magellan's brother-in-law. Not to be confused with Domingo Paes.
Domingo Paes Portugal 1520–1522 CE
Ruler: Krishnadeva Raya (Vijayanagara) Book: Chronica dos reis de Bisnaga Key observations:
  • Compared Hampi to Rome in splendour — the most quoted line about Vijayanagara
  • Mentioned 12,000 female royal guards — a rare detail about women in the Vijayanagara military
  • Noted military organisation, agriculture, and irrigation — the Tungabhadra canals
Exam keyword: "Which traveller compared Hampi to Rome?" — Domingo Paes. "Who described the 12,000 female guards?" — Domingo Paes.
Fernão Nunes Portugal 1535 CE From timeline
Ruler: Achyuta Raya (Vijayanagara) Occupation: Worked as a writer, astrologer, and accountant in the Vijayanagara empire — a settler, not a passing visitor. Book: Chronica dos reis de Bisnaga — usually treated as a joint chronicle with Domingo Paes' account; Nunes continued it for later reigns. Exam keyword: If the question is about a Portuguese who lived in Vijayanagara as a professional, the answer is Fernão Nunes — not Paes.

5 · Mughal era — the European embassy & travel wave (1583–1668 CE)

Not in the PDF. Added because the Bernier–Tavernier–Roe cluster is a recurring Prelims favourite, especially for "oriental despotism" and the English embassy.

Ralph Fitch England 1583–1591 CE Added
Ruler: Akbar Key fact: The first English traveller to India. Visited Fatehpur Sikri, Agra, and Bengal. His report on the wealth of Indian trade is one of the triggers for the formation of the East India Company (1600).
William Hawkins England 1608–1611 CE Added
Ruler: Jahangir Key fact: The first English ambassador to the Mughal court, sent by the East India Company. Spoke Turkish and Persian; failed to secure the trading rights he sought. His embassy is the first formal English–Mughal contact.
Sir Thomas Roe England 1615–1619 CE Added
Ruler: Jahangir Key fact: Ambassador of King James I — the most famous English embassy to the Mughal court. He secured a limited farman for trade in Gujarat, but far less than the Company wanted. His journal is a primary source for Jahangir's court, the emperor's daily routine, and Mughal ceremonial.
François Bernier France 1656–1668 CE Added
Rulers: Aurangzeb, and earlier Dara Shikoh Book: Travels in the Mogul Empire (Histoire de la grande guerre) Why he is famous:
  • His account is the source of the "oriental despotism" theory — the idea that the Mughal state owned all land and there was no private property
  • He argued that this absence of private property was the reason India had not developed like Europe — an argument later picked up by Montesquieu and Marx
  • Described the Mughal court, the mansabdari system, the state of agriculture, and the devastating famine of 1660–61
Exam keyword: Bernier = oriental despotism + no private property in land. This pairing is a permanent Prelims trap.
Jean-Baptiste Tavernier France 1638–1663 CE Added
Rulers: Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb Book: Les Six Voyages Key fact: A gem merchant who made six voyages to India. His account of the diamond trade — the Golconda mines, the great diamonds of the Mughal treasury — is unmatched by any other European source.
Niccolao Manucci Italy 1656–1717 CE Added
Ruler: Aurangzeb Book: Storia do Mogor Key fact: A self-taught Venetian who arrived as a soldier and lived in India for over 60 years. His account of Aurangzeb's court, the Deccan wars, and the Mughal nobility is the most intimate of all the European travelogues.

6 · The modern colonial gaze (1784 – 1900s)

Not in the PDF. Added because these are the writers who created the India that the freedom movement then had to argue with — and they appear in questions on colonial historiography.

William Jones England 1784 CE Added
Key fact: Founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal (Calcutta, 1784) — the institutional beginning of Orientalist scholarship in India. Translated: Shakuntala, Gita Govinda, and the Manusmriti (with Colebrooke). His work created the textual India that the West then argued about. Exam keyword: "Who founded the Asiatic Society?" — William Jones, 1784.
James Mill England 1817 CE Added
Book: History of British India (1817) The single most-asked fact: Mill never visited India — he wrote the book entirely from London, from other people's papers. Why he matters: Introduced the tripartite periodisation of Indian history into Hindu → Muslim → British periods. This periodisation was later adopted into Indian school textbooks and continues to be debated. Exam trap: The question usually asks who introduced the periodisation of Indian history into Hindu–Muslim–British. It is James Mill — not an Indian historian, and not a traveller who set foot in India.
James Prinsep England 1837 CE Added
Key fact: Deciphered Ashokan Brahmi and Kharosthi in 1837 — the single most important epigraphic breakthrough in Indian history. Why it matters: Before Prinsep, Ashoka was known only from Buddhist texts. After him, the Mauryan Empire became a documented period. Every question about the "rediscovery of Ashoka" ends at Prinsep.
Alexander Cunningham England 1861 CE Added
Key fact: First Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), founded 1861. Known as the "Father of Indian Archaeology". His work: Identified and documented Buddhist sites across northern India — Sanchi, Sarnath, Bodh Gaya — using Xuanzang's and Faxian's accounts as his guide. The Chinese pilgrims, 1,200 years later, helped the British find the sites.
Max Müller Germany / England 1875 onward Added
Key fact: Editor of the Sacred Books of the East (50 volumes) — the first systematic English translation of Indian, Persian, and Chinese religious texts. Also: Produced the first critical edition of the Rigveda with Sayana's commentary. His work shaped how the West — and, in reaction, the Indian reform movements — understood "Hinduism".

7 · Memory tricks that save you

The two Chinese, in one line. "Xuanzang 5 up, Yijing 10 down"
Xuanzang came overland ("up" through Central Asia), studied 5 years at Nalanda, carried 657 texts back. Yijing came by sea ("down" via the Bay of Bengal), stayed 10 years, and translated the texts. Five up, ten down — you'll never mix them again.
The Vijayanagara six, in chronological order. "Conti, Razzaq, Nikitin, Barbosa, Paes, Nunes"
Read the years as a ladder: 1419 · 1442 · 1469 · 1500 · 1520 · 1535. The three pre-Portuguese visitors are Conti (Italian, disguised), Razzaq (Persian envoy), and Nikitin (Russian merchant). The three Portuguese are Barbosa (diamonds), Paes (Rome comparison), Nunes (long-term resident). Three before, three after — the Portuguese arrival in 1498 is the hinge.
Ibn Battuta — the Qazi fact. "Battuta judged, Tughlaq ruled"
Muhammad bin Tughlaq appointed a Moroccan as Qazi of Delhi — a foreigner running the Sultanate's judicial system. This one appointment is the single most-asked foreign-traveller fact in Indian history papers. If the question pairs a traveller with a job title, it is almost always this one.
Bernier vs Tavernier vs Manucci — the three French-era Europeans. "Bernier theorised, Tavernier traded, Manucci stayed"
Bernier → oriental despotism, no private property, theories that fed Montesquieu and Marx. Tavernier → Six Voyages, the diamond trade, Golconda. Manucci → Storia do Mogor, 60 years in India, the intimate insider's view. Theory, trade, time — that's the sort.
Al-Biruni — the odd one out. "Biruni came with the conqueror, but wrote like a scholar"
He arrived in Mahmud of Ghazni's baggage train — but his Kitab-ul-Hind is the only pre-modern foreign account that learned Sanskrit, studied the Vedas, and used a comparative method. Every other traveller of his era was a merchant or a pilgrim. That contrast is the exam point.
The English embassy sequence. "Fitch first, Hawkins half, Roe royal"
Ralph Fitch (1583) — the first Englishman in India, under Akbar. William Hawkins (1608) — the first English ambassador, under Jahangir, half-successful. Sir Thomas Roe (1615) — ambassador of King James I, also under Jahangir, the famous one. Fitch first, Hawkins half, Roe royal.
James Mill — the trap that catches half the aspirants. "Mill never went to Mill-land"
James Mill wrote the History of British India (1817) — the book that introduced the Hindu–Muslim–British periodisation — without ever visiting India. If a question asks "which of these travellers never visited India?", Mill is the answer. Do not confuse him with William Jones, who did come, founded the Asiatic Society, and translated Shakuntala.
Two corrections to the notes you shared. (1) Marco Polo's dates and location — he was in South India around 1292–1294, at the Pandya court, on his way home from China. He did not visit the Delhi Sultanate. (2) Fernão Nunes arrived in 1535 during the reign of Achyuta Raya, not Krishnadeva Raya. Krishnadeva Raya died in 1529. His chronicle continues Domingo Paes' Chronica dos reis de Bisnaga. Do not merge the two Portuguese visitors.

8 · Rapid Fire

Cover the whole page. Answer out loud before you tap.

1. Who was the first Greek eyewitness to India, and what did he write?

Megasthenes — ambassador of Seleucus Nicator at Chandragupta Maurya's court, c. 300 BCE. Wrote Indica. Described seven classes and famously claimed there was no slavery in India.

2. Which Chinese pilgrim described the Gupta administration as mild and liberal, with no capital punishment?

Faxian (Fa-Hien), 399–414 CE, during Chandragupta II Vikramaditya's reign. Book: Record of Buddhist Kingdoms.

3. How long did Xuanzang study at Nalanda, and how many texts did he carry back?

5 years at Nalanda, carried 657 Buddhist texts to China. Book: Si-Yu-Ki. He described Harshavardhana's reign and the Prayag Assembly.

4. Which Chinese traveller came to India by sea and stayed 10 years at Nalanda?

Yijing (I-Tsing), c. 671–695 CE. He translated Sanskrit works into Chinese. Xuanzang came overland and stayed 5 years; Yijing came by sea and stayed 10.

5. Which Arab traveller called the Gurjara-Pratihara king "Ballhara"?

Sulaiman, c. 851 CE. Book: Kitab al-Masalik wa al-Mamalik. He called Bengal "Ruhma" (the Pala kingdom) and Rashtrakuta territory "Ruhimi".

6. Who wrote Kitab-ul-Hind, and under whose patronage?

Al-Biruni (Uzbekistan, 1017–1025 CE), under Mahmud of Ghazni. He learned Sanskrit, studied the Vedas and Puranas, and used a scientific comparative method. He noted Hindus excelled in science but were socially conservative, and criticised the Brahmin reluctance to share knowledge.

7. Which traveller's account is the source of the "oriental despotism" theory, and when did he visit India?

François Bernier (France), 1656–1668 CE, during Aurangzeb's reign. Book: Travels in the Mogul Empire. His argument that the Mughal state owned all land and there was no private property was later picked up by Montesquieu and Marx.

8. Who was appointed Qazi of Delhi by Muhammad bin Tughlaq, and what is his book called?

Ibn Battuta (Morocco), 1333–1347 CE. Book: Rihla. He described the Sultan as capable but unpredictable, and noted harsh punishments, the token currency experiment, the postal system, and slavery.

9. Which traveller visited Vijayanagara before the Portuguese arrived, and how did he travel?

Niccolò Conti (Italy), 1419–1439 CE, during Deva Raya I and II. He travelled disguised as a Muslim merchant. He gave a rare early European description of the city — well before the Portuguese reached India (1498).

10. Which Persian envoy described the Vijayanagara royal procession in detail, and what is his book?

Abdur Razzaq, 1442–1445 CE, during Deva Raya II's reign. Book: Matla-us-Sadain wa Majma-ul-Bahrain. He first halted at Calicut under the Zamorin, then went on to Vijayanagara. Sent by Shah Rukh Mirza of Persia.

11. Which traveller compared Hampi to Rome, and what other detail is he famous for?

Domingo Paes (Portugal), 1520–1522 CE, during Krishnadeva Raya's reign. He compared Hampi to Rome and mentioned 12,000 female royal guards. He also described military organisation, agriculture, and irrigation.

12. Which Portuguese traveller explicitly mentioned the diamond mines of the Vijayanagara region?

Duarte Barbosa, 1500–1516 CE, during Vira Narasimha's reign. Book: Book of Duarte Barbosa. He also described coastal trade networks and Malabar society.

13. Which Russian traveller came to the Bahmani Sultanate, and what is his account called?

Afanasy Nikitin (1469–1472 CE). Book: Voyage Beyond Three Seas. One of the earliest Russian accounts of India.

14. What was Marco Polo's connection to India, and which region did he visit?

He visited the Coromandel Coast (Pandya kingdom) in 1292–1294 CE, on his way home from China. Book: The Travels of Marco Polo. He described the pearl fisheries, climate, and coastal social life. He did not visit North India or the Delhi Sultanate.

15. Who was the first English traveller to India, and under which Mughal emperor?

Ralph Fitch, 1583–1591 CE, under Akbar. He visited Fatehpur Sikri, Agra, and Bengal. His report helped trigger the formation of the East India Company in 1600.

16. Who was the first English ambassador to the Mughal court, and under which emperor?

William Hawkins, 1608–1611 CE, under Jahangir. Sent by the East India Company; he failed to secure the trading rights he wanted.

17. Who was Sir Thomas Roe, and which king sent him to India?

Roe was the ambassador of King James I to Jahangir's court (1615–1619 CE). He secured a limited farman for trade in Gujarat — the most famous English embassy to the Mughals.

18. Which French traveller made six voyages to India and wrote about the diamond trade?

Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, 1638–1663 CE, during Shah Jahan's and Aurangzeb's reigns. Book: Les Six Voyages. His account of the Golconda diamond mines is unmatched.

19. Who wrote Storia do Mogor, and what makes his account unusual?

Niccolao Manucci (Italy), 1656–1717 CE. He arrived as a soldier and lived in India for over 60 years — making his account the most intimate European record of Aurangzeb's court and the Deccan wars.

20. Who founded the Asiatic Society of Bengal, when, and where?

William Jones, 1784, Calcutta. He translated Shakuntala, Gita Govinda, and the Manusmriti (with Colebrooke).

21. Who wrote the History of British India, and what is the most important thing to remember about him?

James Mill, 1817 — and he never visited India. He introduced the tripartite periodisation of Indian history into Hindu → Muslim → British periods.

22. Who deciphered Ashokan Brahmi, and in which year?

James Prinsep, 1837. This single breakthrough made the Mauryan Empire a documented historical period — before him, Ashoka was known only from Buddhist texts.

23. Who was the first Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India, and what is he called?

Alexander Cunningham, ASI founded 1861. Known as the "Father of Indian Archaeology". He used Xuanzang's and Faxian's accounts to locate Buddhist sites like Sanchi and Sarnath.

24. Who edited the Sacred Books of the East, and what else is he famous for?

Max Müller. The Sacred Books of the East ran to 50 volumes. He also produced the first critical edition of the Rigveda with Sayana's commentary.
