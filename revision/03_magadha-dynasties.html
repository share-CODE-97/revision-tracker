Ancient Indian Dynasties — Magadha to Guptas

544 BCE → 550 CE. Eight dynasties, one thousand years, and the single most-asked cluster in Prelims Ancient India.

How to use this page: every card is closed by default. Tap it — or hit Reveal all — only after you have tried to recall the king, the date, and the title. Reading an open card teaches you nothing. Guessing first is what makes it stick.
How this topic is actually asked. Almost every Prelims question falls into one of five buckets:
① Title → king (who was "Devanampriya Priyadarshi"?)
② King → dynasty / date (Bimbisara belonged to which dynasty?)
③ Chronological order (who came first — Haryanka or Shishunaga?)
④ Capital / founder / successor (who founded Pataliputra?)
⑤ Foreign / Greek names (Sandrakottos = Chandragupta Maurya)

1 · The Pre-Mauryan stage — Haryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda

544–321 BCE. Magadha's rise from a small kingdom in Bihar to the first great empire of India.

Haryanka Dynasty Pre-Maurya 544–413 BCE
Founder: Bimbisara (544–492 BCE) — title Seniya. Capital: Rajgriha (Rajgir). Significance: The first dynasty to make Magadha a major power. Contemporary of Gautama Buddha and Mahavira — the Haryankas ruled during the lifetime of both. Key kings: Bimbisara → Ajatashatru → Udayin → Anuruddha, Munda, Nagadasaka.
Bimbisara (544–492 BCE) — "Seniya" V.Imp
① Founder of the Haryanka dynasty — the first historical dynasty of Magadha.
② Contemporary of Buddha and Mahavira — both are said to have visited his court.
③ Matrimonial alliances: Married Kosala Devi (princess of Kosala), Chellana (Lichchhavi princess of Vaishali), and Khema (princess of Madra). This is why he is called the first Indian king to use matrimonial alliances as a tool of diplomacy.
④ Title "Seniya" means "of the army" — a reference to his military strength.
⑤ Capital: Rajgriha. He built the city's first fortifications.
Ajatashatru (492–460 BCE) — "Killer of Father" V.Imp
① Killed his father Bimbisara and seized the throne — this is why his title in Jain and Buddhist sources is Kunika ("the parricide").
② Contemporary of Buddha — the Buddha died during his reign (c. 483 BCE). The First Buddhist Council was held at Rajgriha under his patronage.
③ Wars: Fought against Kosala (his maternal uncle's kingdom) and against the Lichchhavis of Vaishali — the latter was a long-drawn war.
④ Military innovations: Used rathamusala (a scythed chariot) and mahashilakantaka (a stone-throwing catapult) against the Lichchhavis.
⑤ Moved the capital to Pataliputra — the foundation of what would become one of the greatest cities of the ancient world.
⑥ Also known as: "Videhi" and "Vaidehi" in some sources (because of his mother's Kosalan lineage).
Udayin (460–444 BCE) V.Imp
① Founded Pataliputra — the future capital of the Mauryas, the Shungas, the Guptas, and the British (as Patna). One of the most strategic city-foundations in Indian history.
② Reason for the shift: Pataliputra was at the confluence of the Ganga and Son rivers — far easier to defend than Rajgriha and better placed for trade and communication.
③ Killed by the successors — after Udayin, the Haryanka dynasty fell into a period of weak rulers.
Shishunaga Dynasty Pre-Maurya 413–345 BCE
Founder: Shishunaga (413–395 BCE) — a former amatya (minister) who overthrew the last Haryanka ruler. Key king: Kalashoka (395–367 BCE) — also called Kakavarna. Capital: Rajgriha, later shifted to Pataliputra (Vaishali for a brief period under Kalashoka). Significance:
  • The Second Buddhist Council was held at Vaishali (c. 383 BCE) under Kalashoka's patronage — where the Buddhist order first split into Sthaviravadins and Mahasanghikas.
  • Kalashoka divided the kingdom among his sons — a partition that weakened Magadha and paved the way for the Nandas.
  • Shishunaga temporarily shifted the capital to Vaishali, but Pataliputra remained the effective centre.
Kalashoka (395–367 BCE) — "Kakavarna" V.Imp
① Patron of the Second Buddhist Council at Vaishali (c. 383 BCE) — the council that formalised the first great schism in Buddhism.
② Divided his kingdom among his ten sons — this partition is often cited as the beginning of the end for the Shishunagas.
③ "Kakavarna" means "crow-coloured" — a nickname given in the Puranas.
Nanda Dynasty Pre-Maurya 345–321 BCE
Founder: Mahapadma Nanda (345–329 BCE) — the first Shudra king of Magadha, and the first "empire-builder" of India. Last ruler: Dhana Nanda (329–321 BCE) — overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya with the help of Chanakya (Kautilya). Significance:
  • First dynasty to extend Magadha's power across almost the whole of northern India — from the Punjab to Bengal, and from the Himalayas to the Godavari.
  • The Nandas were enormously wealthy — their treasury was one of the reasons Alexander's soldiers wanted to march to the Ganga (they refused, and Alexander turned back).
  • Described in Greek sources as ruling over a vast army: 200,000 infantry, 20,000 cavalry, 2,000 four-horse chariots, 3,000 elephants (per Diodorus and Curtius).
Mahapadma Nanda (345–329 BCE) — "Ekarat" V.Imp
① Title "Ekarat" = "sole sovereign" — the first Indian ruler to be called the sole sovereign of a vast territory.
② Also called "Sarva-kshatrantaka" — "destroyer of all Kshatriyas" — because he is said to have wiped out the old Kshatriya dynasties of northern India.
③ First Shudra king of Magadha, per the Puranas — a major social break from the Haryankas and Shishunagas.
④ His army was the largest India had seen until then — and the fear of it is what made Alexander's army refuse to advance beyond the Beas.
⑤ His empire is the territorial base that Chandragupta Maurya inherited and expanded.
Dhana Nanda (329–321 BCE) V.Imp
① Last Nanda ruler. Overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya (with Chanakya's strategy) in c. 321 BCE — the founding date of the Mauryan Empire.
② Called "Agrammes" or "Xandrammes" in Greek sources (probably a corruption of "Dhana Nanda" or a title).
③ His unpopularity — heavy taxation, low status in the varna order, and a reputation for greed — is what made Chanakya's plot succeed.
④ Chanakya's grudge: According to tradition, Chanakya was insulted by Dhana Nanda at his court, and swore to destroy the Nanda dynasty. This is the backdrop of the Arthashastra and the Mudrarakshasa.

2 · The Mauryan Empire — the first all-India state

321–185 BCE. The dynasty that gave us Chandragupta, Chanakya, Megasthenes, Ashoka, and the first pan-Indian administration.

Maurya Dynasty Maurya 321–185 BCE
Founder: Chandragupta Maurya (321–297 BCE) — with Chanakya (Kautilya / Vishnugupta). Capital: Pataliputra (modern Patna). Key kings: Chandragupta → Bindusara → Ashoka → Dasharatha → Samprati → Salisuka, Devavarman, Satadhanvan, Brihadratha. Significance: The first dynasty to unify almost the entire Indian subcontinent — from the Hindu Kush to the Godavari. Also the first to leave behind a coherent administrative text (the Arthashastra) and a rich epigraphic record (Ashoka's edicts).
Chandragupta Maurya (321–297 BCE) — "Sandrakottos" V.Imp
① Founder of the Mauryan Empire — the first empire in Indian history to cover almost the whole subcontinent.
② Greek name: Sandrakottos (also written Sandrocottus). This is how the Greeks — and through them, the Romans — knew him.
③ Defeated Seleucus Nicator (Alexander's general) in c. 305 BCE. The treaty gave Chandragupta: (a) the satrapies of Arachosia, Gedrosia, and Paropamisadae; (b) a marriage alliance; (c) Megasthenes as Seleucus' ambassador to the Mauryan court.
④ Megasthenes wrote Indica — the first Greek account of India, and our main foreign source for Mauryan administration.
⑤ Chanakya (Kautilya) was his chief minister and the author of the Arthashastra — the foundational text of Indian statecraft.
⑥ Jain tradition: In his later years Chandragupta converted to Jainism, went south with Bhadrabahu, and died by Sallekhana (ritual fasting) at Shravanabelagola (Karnataka).
⑦ His empire — from the Hindu Kush and Baluchistan in the west to Bengal in the east, and from the Himalayas to the Deccan — is the territorial template for the Guptas.
Bindusara (297–273 BCE) — "Amitraghata" V.Imp
① Title "Amitraghata" = "Slayer of Foes". The Greeks called him Amitrochates.
② Son of Chandragupta, father of Ashoka. His reign is the least documented of the three great Mauryas — because he left no inscriptions of his own.
③ Known for: consolidating the empire his father had built, putting down revolts in Taxila and Ujjain, and maintaining diplomatic contact with the Greek world (Deimachus was the Seleucid ambassador at his court).
④ Jain tradition: He was a patron of Jainism and is said to have been a follower of the Jain saint Pingalavatsa.
⑤ His death (c. 273 BCE) was followed by a four-year war of succession — Ashoka emerged as the victor.
Ashoka the Great (273–232 BCE) — "Devanampriya Priyadarshi" V.Imp
① Titles: Devanampriya ("Beloved of the Gods") and Priyadarshi ("He who looks on with affection"). These are the names Ashoka uses for himself in his edicts — not "Ashoka", which appears only in a few minor inscriptions.
② The Kalinga War (c. 261 BCE) — the turning point of his life. The bloodshed (per his own edict: 100,000 killed, 150,000 deported) led him to embrace Buddhism and the policy of Dhamma.
③ Dhamma: Not Buddhism itself, but a set of ethical principles — compassion, truthfulness, tolerance of all sects, non-injury to living beings, and obedience to parents and teachers. His Dhamma Mahamatta officers were appointed to spread it.
④ Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra (c. 250 BCE), under the presidency of Moggaliputta Tissa. This council compiled the Abhidhamma Pitaka and sent missionaries abroad — including Mahendra and Sanghamitra to Sri Lanka.
⑤ His inscriptions: The Rock Edicts (Major and Minor), the Pillar Edicts, and the Separate Edicts. They are in Prakrit using Brahmi script (and Kharosthi in the northwest, Greek and Aramaic in the border regions). The Maski and Gujarra edicts use the name "Ashoka" directly.
⑥ Language of the edicts: Prakrit — the language of the common people, not Sanskrit. This is a deliberate choice for a mass audience.
⑦ His Dhamma tours: He visited Lumbini (Buddha's birthplace, 249 BCE) and erected a pillar there with a reduced tax for the village.
⑧ His death (232 BCE) was followed by the rapid decline of the Mauryan Empire.
V.V.Imp — Ashoka's edicts and what they are NOT: The edicts are not "Buddhist propaganda". They are a public moral code — Ashoka's Dhamma is a civil religion, not a sectarian one. His inscriptions never mention the Buddha, the Sangha, or the Four Noble Truths by name. Questions often try to equate "Dhamma" with "Buddhism" — do not fall for it.
Dasharatha (232–224 BCE) V.Imp
① Grandson of Ashoka, son of either Kunala or Jalauka (sources differ).
② Known for: His inscription on the Nagarjuni Hills (near Barabar, Bihar) — where he dedicated three caves to the Ajivika sect. This is the only Mauryan inscription after Ashoka that names a king directly.
③ Under him the Mauryan Empire began to break up — Vidarbha and parts of the Deccan broke away.
Samprati (224–215 BCE) V.Imp
① Successor of Dasharatha — and one of the most important Jain patrons in Indian history.
② Jain tradition: Called Jaina Ashoka — he is said to have built thousands of Jain temples and sent missionaries to South India and Sri Lanka.
③ His reign is the last period of Mauryan strength in the south; after him the empire rapidly shrinks.
Brihadratha (185 BCE) — the last Maurya V.Imp
① Last Mauryan ruler. Assassinated by his own commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga, during a military review — the founding act of the Shunga dynasty.
② Preceded by: Salisuka, Devavarman, Satadhanvan — all weak rulers who presided over the empire's contraction.
③ Exam keyword: "Who killed the last Mauryan ruler?" — Pushyamitra Shunga, 185 BCE. This is a permanent one-line answer.

3 · The Shunga and Kanva dynasties — the post-Mauryan aftermath

185–28 BCE. Two Brahminical dynasties that ruled Magadha after the Mauryas.

Shunga Dynasty Post-Maurya 185–73 BCE
Founder: Pushyamitra Shunga (185–149 BCE) — a Brahmin general who assassinated Brihadratha. Capital: Pataliputra, with Vidisha (Besnagar) as an important secondary centre. Key kings: Pushyamitra → Agnimitra → Vasujyeshtha → Bhagabhadra → Devabhuti. Significance:
  • A Brahminical reaction to the Mauryan patronage of Buddhism and Jainism — the Shungas revived Vedic sacrifices, especially the Ashvamedha.
  • Pushyamitra is credited with two Ashvamedha sacrifices.
  • Patanjali's Mahabhashya (c. 150 BCE) refers to Pushyamitra's Ashvamedha — one of the few contemporary references.
  • The Shunga empire extended from the Punjab to the Narmada, with Vidisha as a key centre.
  • After Pushyamitra, the dynasty declined rapidly — Devabhuti, the last ruler, was killed by his own minister Vasudeva Kanva.
Pushyamitra Shunga (185–149 BCE) V.Imp
① Assassinated Brihadratha — the last Mauryan ruler — during a military review, and founded the Shunga dynasty.
② A Brahmin by birth — this is significant because it marks the return of Brahminical power after the Shudra Nandas and the Buddhist-leaning Mauryas.
③ Performed two Ashvamedha sacrifices — a public statement of Vedic revival.
④ Buddhist sources (especially the Divyavadana) accuse him of persecuting Buddhists — but this is debated by historians. The evidence is mixed.
⑤ His court was a centre of Sanskrit learning — Patanjali's Mahabhashya belongs to this period.
⑥ His son Agnimitra is the hero of Kalidasa's play Malavikagnimitram.
Kanva Dynasty Post-Maurya 73–28 BCE
Founder: Vasudeva Kanva (73–64 BCE) — a minister of the last Shunga ruler, Devabhuti. He killed his master and seized the throne. Key kings: Vasudeva → Bhumimitra → Narayana → Susarman. Significance:
  • A short-lived dynasty (about 45 years) — ruled only over Magadha and its immediate surroundings.
  • Their power was largely nominal — the real authority in the region had passed to the Satavahanas in the Deccan and the Indo-Greeks in the northwest.
  • The dynasty ended when the Satavahanas overthrew Susarman (c. 28 BCE).
  • They were Brahmins, like the Shungas — and the Puranas record them as the successors of the Shungas in Magadha.

4 · The Satavahanas — the bridge to the Deccan

1st century BCE – 3rd century CE. The first great Deccan power — and the dynasty that links Magadha's legacy to South India.

Satavahana Dynasty Deccan 1st c. BCE – 3rd c. CE
Also called: Andhra dynasty — the Puranas refer to them as Andhras. Founder: Simuka (c. 1st century BCE). Capital: Pratishthana (Paithan, Maharashtra) — with Amaravati and Dhanyakataka as other important centres. Key kings: Simuka → Satakarni I → Gautamiputra Satakarni → Vashishthiputra Pulumavi → Yajna Sri Satakarni. Significance:
  • The first major power in the Deccan — controlled the trade routes between the Gangetic plain and the ports of the west and east coasts.
  • Their coins and inscriptions are the first to be issued in the Deccan with Sanskrit and Prakrit legends.
  • Known as the bridge dynasty — they carried the Magadhan political and cultural legacy into South India.
  • Patrons of both Buddhism and Brahminism — they gave land grants to both, which is a key marker of the early medieval transition.
Gautamiputra Satakarni (106–130 CE) — "Trisamudratoyapitavahana" V.Imp
① The greatest Satavahana ruler — his empire covered the Deccan from the Konkan to the Godavari, and from Malwa to the Krishna delta.
② Title "Trisamudratoyapitavahana" = "one whose horses drank the waters of the three oceans" — a claim to suzerainty over a vast area between the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal, and the Indian Ocean (this is usually interpreted as a reference to the three coasts).
③ Defeated Nahapana, the Western Kshatrapa ruler — a victory recorded in his mother's Nashik inscription (the Nasik prasasti by Gautami Balashri).
④ His mother Gautami Balashri — one of the most important women in early Indian epigraphy. Her inscription is the main source for his reign.
⑤ He is described in the inscription as the "destroyer of Shakas, Yavanas, Pahlavas" — a claim of defeating foreign powers.
⑥ Also known as a patron of Brahminism — he is described as Eka-Brahmana ("the only Brahmana"), meaning a great patron of the Brahmanas.
Vashishthiputra Pulumavi (130–154 CE) V.Imp
① Son of Gautamiputra Satakarni — his name means "son of Vashishthi" (his mother's name).
② Known for: Consolidating the Satavahana empire in the Deccan after his father's conquests. His coins and inscriptions are found across Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh.
③ The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (1st century CE) mentions the Satavahana ports — this period sees the peak of Indo-Roman trade.
④ His inscriptions record donations to Buddhist monasteries — showing the continuing Satavahana patronage of Buddhism.
Yajna Sri Satakarni (165–194 CE) V.Imp
① One of the last great Satavahana rulers. His coins depict a ship with a mast — the most direct numismatic evidence of Satavahana maritime trade.
② His reign marks the last phase of Satavahana power before the dynasty fragmented into smaller Deccan kingdoms.
③ Exam keyword: The ship-type coins of Yajna Sri Satakarni are a classic "match the coin to the dynasty" question.

5 · The Kushans — the empire that gave India the Saka era

1st century CE – 3rd century CE. A Central Asian dynasty that ruled from Afghanistan to the Ganga — and defined the art and chronology of early India.

Kushan Dynasty Central Asia 1st c. CE – 3rd c. CE
Also called: Kushana or Yuezhi — a branch of the Yuezhi confederation from Central Asia. Founder (of the Indian branch): Kujula Kadphises (30–80 CE). Capital: Purushapura (Peshawar) — with a summer capital at Mathura. Key kings: Kujula Kadphises → Vima Kadphises → Kanishka → Huvishka → Vasudeva I. Significance:
  • Controlled the Silk Road — the trade route between China, India, Persia, and the Roman Empire. Their coins and art show a fusion of Indian, Greek, Persian, and Central Asian elements.
  • The Gandhara school of art and the Mathura school flourished under Kushan patronage.
  • The first gold coins in India were issued by Vima Kadphises — the Kushans are the first to mint gold on a large scale in India.
  • The Saka era (78 CE) — still used by the Government of India as the national calendar — was started by Kanishka.
Kanishka (127–150 CE) — the greatest Kushan V.Imp
① Started the Saka era (78 CE) — the official Indian national calendar is still based on this era. Every Indian government calendar uses Saka dates.
② Fourth Buddhist Council was held in Kashmir (Kundalvana, near Srinagar) under his patronage — presided over by Vasumitra and Ashvaghosha. This council formally split Buddhism into Mahayana and Hinayana.
③ Patron of Mahayana Buddhism — he is called the "Second Ashoka" for his patronage of Buddhism, though his was specifically Mahayana.
④ His court was a gathering of intellectual giants — Ashvaghosha (poet of the Buddhacharita), Vasumitra, Charaka (the physician, author of the Charaka Samhita), and Nagarjuna (the philosopher).
⑤ His conquests: He extended Kushan power over Kashmir, the Punjab, and parts of the Ganga plain — and fought a war with the Han Chinese (recorded in Chinese sources).
⑥ His coins show a remarkable range of deities — Buddha, Shiva, Iranian gods, and Greek gods — a sign of the religious plurality of his empire.
⑦ His relics — the famous Kanishka casket — are held in Peshawar Museum; his statue is in Mathura Museum.
⑧ His name in Chinese sources is Qiujiuque or Jia-ni-se-jia. Inscriptions call him Maharaja Rajadhiraja Devaputra Kanishka.
V.V.Imp — the Saka era: The Saka era began in 78 CE and is traditionally attributed to Kanishka. The Indian National Calendar (adopted 22 March 1957) uses the Saka era — the first month is Chaitra, and the year begins on 22 March (21 March in a leap year). A permanent Prelims question.
Vima Kadphises (95–127 CE) V.Imp
① First Kushan ruler to issue gold coins — the first large-scale gold coinage in India. This is a direct consequence of the Silk Road trade.
② His coins show a remarkable fusion: the king on one side, and on the other, Oesho (a form of Shiva), Iranian deities, or Greek gods.
③ His reign marks the territorial consolidation of the Kushan empire in India — from Bactria to the Ganga plain.
Vasudeva I (190–230 CE) — the last great Kushan V.Imp
① The last great Kushan ruler — after him the empire fragmented into smaller Kushano-Sasanian and local dynasties.
② His coins are predominantly Shaivite — the reverse shows Shiva with a bull. This marks a shift from the earlier Buddhist-and-eclectic coinage.
③ His name itself — "Vasudeva" — is a Vaishnava name, indicating the growing strength of Brahminical Hinduism in the Kushan court.
④ End of the Kushans in India: their territory was taken over by the Nagas, the Yaudheyas, and eventually the Guptas.

6 · The Guptas — the golden age

c. 240–550 CE. The dynasty that defined classical India — its art, its literature, its science, and its political ideal.

Gupta Dynasty Golden Age c. 240–550 CE
Founder: Sri Gupta (c. 240–280 CE) — probably a local chief in Magadha. Capital: Pataliputra, with Ujjain as a major secondary centre (especially under Chandragupta II). Key kings: Sri Gupta → Ghatotkacha → Chandragupta I → Samudragupta → Chandragupta II → Kumaragupta I → Skandagupta. Significance:
  • The empire that gave India the classical age — the age of Kalidasa, Aryabhata, Varahamihira, and the Ajanta frescoes.
  • The Gupta era (319–320 CE) was started by Chandragupta I — the second great era after the Saka era.
  • The empire at its peak covered almost the whole of northern India — from the Indus to the Brahmaputra, and from the Himalayas to the Narmada.
  • The decline began with the Huna invasions — Skandagupta checked them, but his successors could not.
Chandragupta I (319–335 CE) — "Maharajadhiraja" V.Imp
① First great Gupta ruler — he raised the family from local chiefs to imperial status.
② Started the Gupta era (319–320 CE) — the second great era in Indian chronology, after the Saka era. The date is usually taken as 26 February 320 CE (his accession).
③ Married Kumaradevi — a Lichchhavi princess. This marriage gave the Guptas the prestige of the Lichchhavi lineage, which they advertised on their coins (the "Lichchhavi" type coins show the couple).
④ Title "Maharajadhiraja" — "Great King of Kings" — the first Gupta to use this imperial title.
⑤ His empire covered Magadha, Saketa (Ayodhya), and Prayaga (Allahabad) — the core of what Samudragupta would later expand.
Samudragupta (335–375 CE) — "Napoleon of India" V.Imp
① Called the "Napoleon of India" by the British historian V. A. Smith — because of his extensive conquests and military genius.
② His campaigns are recorded in the Allahabad Pillar Inscription (Prayag Prashasti), composed by his court poet Harisena. This is the single most important source for his reign.
③ His conquests: He defeated 9 kings of Aryavarta (northern India), 12 kings of Dakshinapatha (the Deccan), the Atavika (forest) kingdoms, and the frontier kingdoms (Samatata, Davaka, Kamarupa, Nepala, Kartripura). He also received tribute from the Kushans, Shakas, and the kings of Sri Lanka.
④ Title "Kaviraja" — "King of Poets" — he was himself a poet and musician. His coins show him playing the veena. He is also called Parakramanka.
⑤ His Ashvamedha — he performed the Ashvamedha sacrifice to proclaim his sovereignty.
⑥ His coins: The "Ashvamedha" type, the "Lyrist" type (playing the veena), the "Archer" type, and the "Battle-axe" type — a remarkable numismatic record.
⑦ He was succeeded by his son Chandragupta II — the two together are the high point of the Gupta empire.
Chandragupta II (375–415 CE) — "Vikramaditya" V.Imp
① Title "Vikramaditya" — "Sun of Valour" — the name by which he is best known. The legend of Vikramaditya and the Navaratna (nine gems) is traditionally associated with his court.
② The Mehrauli Iron Pillar (Delhi) — the famous rust-free iron pillar — carries an inscription that is usually attributed to Chandragupta II (or to a king named Chandra). The pillar is a metallurgical marvel — the iron has not rusted in 1,600 years.
③ Defeated the Western Kshatrapas — his campaign against Rudrasimha III ended Shaka rule in Gujarat and gave the Guptas control of the western ports (a major economic gain).
④ Married Kuberanaga — a Naga princess — to secure the Naga territories.
⑤ The Navaratna: Kalidasa, Dhanvantari, Kshapanaka, Amarasimha, Shanku, Vetalabhatta, Ghatakarpara, Varahamihira, and Vararuchi — nine gems of his court (the list varies). Kalidasa is the most famous.
⑥ His court was the peak of Sanskrit literature — Kalidasa wrote Abhijnanashakuntalam, Meghaduta, Raghuvamsha, Kumarasambhava, and Ritusamhara.
⑦ The Chinese pilgrim Faxian (Fa-Hien) visited India during his reign (399–414 CE) — his account is our main foreign source for Gupta administration.
⑧ His coins — the "Archer" type is the most famous. He is also shown on a coin with his queen.
V.V.Imp — the Navaratna of Chandragupta II: Kalidasa (poet), Dhanvantari (physician), Kshapanaka (astrologer), Amarasimha (lexicographer), Shanku (architect), Vetalabhatta (magician), Ghatakarpara (poet), Varahamihira (astronomer), Vararuchi (grammarian). The list varies across sources — but Kalidasa and Varahamihira are always there.
Kumaragupta I (415–455 CE) — "Mahendraditya" V.Imp
① Title "Mahendraditya" — "Sun among the great".
② Founded Nalanda University — the greatest centre of learning in ancient India. This is the single most important fact about him for UPSC.
③ His coins show him as a horseman, a lion-slayer, and an archer — a sign of his military activity.
④ His reign saw the beginning of the Huna attacks — though he seems to have held them off in his lifetime.
⑤ The Bhitari Pillar Inscription of Skandagupta records Kumaragupta's reign and his campaigns.
Skandagupta (455–467 CE) — "Vikramaditya" V.Imp
① The last great Gupta ruler.
② Defeated the Huns (Hunas) — the Central Asian invaders who would eventually destroy the Gupta empire. His Bhitari Pillar Inscription records his victories against the Pushyamitras and the Hunas.
③ The Junagadh Rock Inscription — the same as Rudradaman's earlier inscription — records the repair of the Sudarshana Lake under Skandagupta's governor, Parnadatta.
④ His coins show him as a lion-slayer and a horseman — a clear military iconography.
⑤ After him the Gupta empire shrank rapidly — his successors Purugupta, Narasimhagupta Baladitya, Kumaragupta II, Budhagupta, and Vishnugupta ruled over a much smaller territory until c. 550 CE.
The later Guptas (467–550 CE) V.Imp
① Purugupta (467–473 CE) — son of Kumaragupta I (or of Skandagupta, depending on the source).
② Narasimhagupta Baladitya (c. 495–530 CE) — known for his conflicts with the Hunas. The Chinese traveller Xuanzang refers to him as a patron of Nalanda.
③ Kumaragupta II (c. 473–476 CE) — a short reign.
④ Budhagupta (c. 476–495 CE) — one of the last Guptas with inscriptions.
⑤ Vishnugupta (c. 540–550 CE) — the last recognised Gupta ruler. The empire had shrunk to Magadha and its neighbourhood.
⑥ The end of the Guptas is usually dated to c. 550 CE, when the Maukhari dynasty of Kanauj and the Later Guptas of Magadha emerged as successor states.

7 · Very Important notes for UPSC / BPSC

The one-liners, traps, and "not-to-be-confused" facts that decide the answer.

The Buddhist Councils — dynasty by dynasty V.Imp
First Council — Rajgriha, c. 483 BCE, under Ajatashatru (Haryanka). President: Mahakassapa. Compiled the Vinaya and Sutta Pitakas. Second Council — Vaishali, c. 383 BCE, under Kalashoka (Shishunaga). Resulted in the first great schism — Sthaviravadins vs Mahasanghikas. Third Council — Pataliputra, c. 250 BCE, under Ashoka (Maurya). President: Moggaliputta Tissa. Compiled the Abhidhamma Pitaka. Missionaries sent abroad — Mahendra and Sanghamitra to Sri Lanka. Fourth Council — Kundalvana, Kashmir, c. 1st–2nd century CE, under Kanishka (Kushan). President: Vasumitra. Vice-President: Ashvaghosha. Buddhism split formally into Mahayana and Hinayana. Exam keyword: "Which Buddhist council was held under Kanishka?" — Fourth, Kashmir. "Which was held under Ashoka?" — Third, Pataliputra.
The Greek names of Indian kings V.Imp
Sandrakottos = Chandragupta Maurya (given by Megasthenes and other Greek writers). Amitrochates = Bindusara (from Amitraghata, "Slayer of Foes"). Agrammes / Xandrammes = Dhana Nanda (usually interpreted as a corruption of "Dhana Nanda" or a title). Devanampriya / Priyadarshi = Ashoka (in his own edicts — the name "Ashoka" appears only in a few minor inscriptions). Exam trap: The question usually asks "which Greek name refers to Chandragupta Maurya?" — Sandrakottos. Do not confuse it with "Amitrochates" (Bindusara).
The two eras — Saka and Gupta V.Imp
Saka era — started 78 CE, attributed to Kanishka. Used by the Government of India as the Indian National Calendar (adopted 22 March 1957). Gupta era — started 319–320 CE, by Chandragupta I. Also called the Gupta-Valabhi era in some inscriptions. Vikrama era — 57 BCE, associated with the legendary king Vikramaditya of Ujjain (not to be confused with Chandragupta II, who also bore the title Vikramaditya). Exam trap: "Which era is the Indian National Calendar based on?" — the Saka era, not the Gupta era.
Who founded which city V.Imp
Rajgriha — the early capital of Magadha (Haryanka period, Bimbisara). Pataliputra — founded by Udayin (Haryanka dynasty), c. 460 BCE. Later the capital of the Mauryas, Shungas, and Guptas. Vaishali — the Lichchhavi capital; briefly the Shishunaga capital under Kalashoka. Pratishthana (Paithan) — the Satavahana capital. Purushapura (Peshawar) — the Kushan capital, under Kanishka. Ujjain — a major Gupta centre under Chandragupta II. Exam trap: Pataliputra was founded by Udayin — not by Ajatashatru and not by Chandragupta Maurya.
Who wrote what — the sources V.Imp
Indica — Megasthenes (Greek ambassador at Chandragupta Maurya's court). The main foreign source for Mauryan administration. Arthashastra — Kautilya / Chanakya (Chandragupta Maurya's minister). The foundational text of Indian statecraft. Mudrarakshasa — Vishakhadatta (a play about Chanakya's plot against the Nandas). Allahabad Pillar Inscription (Prayag Prashasti) — Harisena, court poet of Samudragupta. The main source for Samudragupta's conquests. Bhitari Pillar Inscription — Skandagupta's reign and his victories over the Hunas. Junagadh Rock Inscription — Rudradaman (Shaka) and Skandagupta (Gupta) — the same rock carries inscriptions of both dynasties. Mahabhashya — Patanjali, c. 150 BCE, referring to Pushyamitra Shunga's Ashvamedha. Periplus of the Erythraean Sea — an anonymous Greek text (1st century CE) describing Indo-Roman trade, including Satavahana ports.
The "firsts" — one-liners that win marks V.Imp
First historical dynasty of Magadha — Haryanka. First Indian king to use matrimonial alliances as diplomacy — Bimbisara. First Shudra king of Magadha — Mahapadma Nanda. First Indian ruler to be called "Ekarat" (sole sovereign) — Mahapadma Nanda. First empire in Indian history to cover almost the whole subcontinent — Mauryan, under Chandragupta Maurya. First large-scale gold coinage in India — Vima Kadphises (Kushan). First to start an era in India that is still in use — Kanishka (Saka era, 78 CE). First Gupta ruler to take the title "Maharajadhiraja" — Chandragupta I. Founder of Nalanda University — Kumaragupta I (Gupta).
The "not-to-be-confused" pairs Trap Alert
Bimbisara vs Ajatashatru: Bimbisara is the father and founder; Ajatashatru is the son who killed him and moved the capital to Pataliputra. Never swap them. Kalashoka vs Kalidasa: Kalashoka is the Shishunaga king (5th century BCE); Kalidasa is the Gupta poet (4th–5th century CE). Completely different people. Chandragupta Maurya vs Chandragupta I vs Chandragupta II: Maurya is the founder (321 BCE); Chandragupta I is the Gupta founder (319 CE); Chandragupta II is Vikramaditya (375 CE). Three different kings, three different eras. Kanishka vs Kanishka II: Kanishka (127–150 CE) is the great Kushan; later "Kanishka" rulers are minor. Prelims usually means the first one. Pushyamitra Shunga vs Pushyamitra of the Shunga dynasty: Same person — the founder. But do not confuse with the Pushyamitras (a tribe defeated by Skandagupta) — different people, different era. Samudragupta vs Skandagupta: Samudragupta is the conqueror (Allahabad Pillar); Skandagupta is the Huna-defeater (Bhitari Pillar). Both are Guptas — but different generations.
Art and architecture under the Guptas V.Imp
Ajanta caves — the finest paintings of the Gupta period (some earlier, some later). The Mahajanaka Jataka and the Bodhisattva Padmapani are the most famous. Sarnath Buddha — the classic Gupta Buddha image, with a serene expression and a transparent drapery. Dashavatara Temple, Deogarh — the finest Gupta temple, with panels of Vishnu's avatars. Mehrauli Iron Pillar — the rust-free iron pillar, attributed to Chandragupta II. A metallurgical marvel. Nalanda University — founded by Kumaragupta I; became the greatest centre of Buddhist learning in the world. Exam keyword: The Gupta period is called the "Golden Age" of Indian art — but the term is contested. The classical label is more accurate than "golden".

8 · Memory tricks that save you

The three Pre-Mauryan dynasties — in order. "Haryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda"
Read the years as a ladder: 544 → 413 → 345 BCE. Haryanka is the founder-dynasty (Bimbisara, Ajatashatru, Udayin). Shishunaga is the short interlude (Shishunaga, Kalashoka). Nanda is the empire-builder (Mahapadma, Dhana). Three dynasties, one road to the Mauryas.
The three great Mauryas — by title. "Chandragupta Sandrakottos, Bindusara Amitraghata, Ashoka Devanampriya"
Chandragupta = Sandrakottos (Greek) = the founder. Bindusara = Amitraghata / Amitrochates = the consolidator. Ashoka = Devanampriya Priyadarshi = the Dhamma-king. Three kings, three titles, three roles — never swap them.
The Buddhist councils — dynasty by dynasty. "Ajatashatru 1st, Kalashoka 2nd, Ashoka 3rd, Kanishka 4th"
1st — Rajgriha, under Ajatashatru (Haryanka). 2nd — Vaishali, under Kalashoka (Shishunaga). 3rd — Pataliputra, under Ashoka (Maurya). 4th — Kashmir, under Kanishka (Kushan). Four councils, four dynasties — the sequence is the question.
The Gupta line — by achievement. "Sri Gupta founds, Ghatotkacha starts, Chandragupta I eras, Samudragupta conquers, Chandragupta II shines, Kumaragupta teaches, Skandagupta defends"
Sri Gupta = founder. Ghatotkacha = first important ruler. Chandragupta I = Gupta era (319 CE). Samudragupta = conquests (Allahabad Pillar). Chandragupta II = golden age (Kalidasa, Navaratna). Kumaragupta I = Nalanda University. Skandagupta = defeated the Hunas. Seven names, seven roles.
The two great eras — the year that matters. "Saka 78, Gupta 319"
Saka era — 78 CE — Kanishka (Kushan). Gupta era — 319–320 CE — Chandragupta I (Gupta). These are the only two eras you must memorise for Prelims. The Vikrama era (57 BCE) is legendary; the Saka and Gupta eras are historical.
The four great "Vikramadityas". "Chandragupta II is the one that matters"
There are several kings with the title Vikramaditya — the legendary Ujjain king (Vikrama era 57 BCE), Chandragupta II (Gupta, 375–415 CE), Skandagupta (Gupta, 455–467 CE), and later Chalukya and Paramara kings. The one that Prelims means is almost always Chandragupta II — the Navaratna, the Mehrauli Pillar, the Faxian visit.
The two Gupta "Chandraguptas" — easy to mix. "I starts the era, II lives in it"
Chandragupta I (319–335 CE) = starts the Gupta era. He married Kumaradevi (Lichchhavi princess) and took the title Maharajadhiraja. Chandragupta II (375–415 CE) = the golden age — Vikramaditya, Navaratna, Mehrauli Pillar, Faxian's visit. Two kings, same name, different roles.
The Nanda to Maurya transition — one chain. "Dhana Nanda → Chanakya's plot → Chandragupta Maurya"
Dhana Nanda insults Chanakya at his court → Chanakya vows to destroy the Nandas → he finds Chandragupta, trains him, and together they overthrow Dhana Nanda in c. 321 BCE. This is the founding story of the Mauryan Empire — and the plot of the Mudrarakshasa.
Three traps to watch. ① "Dhamma" is not Buddhism — it is Ashoka's civil moral code, and his edicts never mention the Buddha by name. ② Pataliputra was founded by Udayin — not Ajatashatru, not Chandragupta Maurya. ③ The Allahabad Pillar Inscription was composed by Harisena — not by Kalidasa, not by Samudragupta himself. Harisena was Samudragupta's court poet.

9 · Rapid Fire

Cover the whole page. Answer out loud before you tap.

1. Who founded the Haryanka dynasty, and what was his title?

Bimbisara (544–492 BCE) — title Seniya. Capital: Rajgriha. Contemporary of Buddha and Mahavira.

2. Who founded Pataliputra, and in which dynasty?

Udayin (460–444 BCE) of the Haryanka dynasty. He shifted the capital from Rajgriha to Pataliputra — at the confluence of the Ganga and Son rivers.

3. Who killed his father and seized the throne of Magadha, and what were his military innovations?

Ajatashatru (492–460 BCE) — killed Bimbisara. He used rathamusala (scythed chariot) and mahashilakantaka (stone-throwing catapult) against the Lichchhavis of Vaishali. The First Buddhist Council was held under his patronage at Rajgriha.

4. Who founded the Shishunaga dynasty, and which Buddhist council was held under Kalashoka?

Shishunaga (413–395 BCE), a former amatya. The Second Buddhist Council was held at Vaishali (c. 383 BCE) under Kalashoka's patronage — where the Buddhist order split into Sthaviravadins and Mahasanghikas.

5. Who was the first Shudra king of Magadha, and what was his title?

Mahapadma Nanda (345–329 BCE) — title Ekarat ("sole sovereign"). He was also called Sarva-kshatrantaka ("destroyer of all Kshatriyas") and was the first great empire-builder of India.

6. Who was the last Nanda ruler, and who overthrew him?

Dhana Nanda (329–321 BCE). Overthrown by Chandragupta Maurya with the help of Chanakya (Kautilya).

7. Who founded the Mauryan Empire, and what is his Greek name?

Chandragupta Maurya (321–297 BCE). Greek name: Sandrakottos. He defeated Seleucus Nicator in c. 305 BCE and received Megasthenes as ambassador.

8. Who wrote the Indica, and whose court did he visit?

Megasthenes — the Greek ambassador of Seleucus Nicator at the court of Chandragupta Maurya. Indica is the main foreign source for Mauryan administration.

9. Who was Bindusara, and what was his title?

Bindusara (297–273 BCE) — son of Chandragupta Maurya and father of Ashoka. Title: Amitraghata ("Slayer of Foes"), which the Greeks rendered as Amitrochates.

10. What are Ashoka's titles in his own edicts, and what was the Kalinga War?

Ashoka calls himself Devanampriya ("Beloved of the Gods") and Priyadarshi ("He who looks on with affection"). The Kalinga War (c. 261 BCE) — 100,000 killed, 150,000 deported — led him to embrace Buddhism and the policy of Dhamma.

11. Which Buddhist council was held under Ashoka, and who presided over it?

The Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra (c. 250 BCE), presided over by Moggaliputta Tissa. It compiled the Abhidhamma Pitaka and sent missionaries abroad — including Mahendra and Sanghamitra to Sri Lanka.

12. Who killed the last Mauryan ruler, and when?

Pushyamitra Shunga — he assassinated Brihadratha (the last Maurya) in 185 BCE during a military review, and founded the Shunga dynasty.

13. Who were the Shungas, and what was their religious policy?

The Shunga dynasty (185–73 BCE) was founded by Pushyamitra Shunga, a Brahmin general. They represented a Brahminical revival — Pushyamitra performed two Ashvamedha sacrifices. Patanjali's Mahabhashya refers to this. The last Shunga ruler, Devabhuti, was killed by his minister Vasudeva Kanva.

14. Who founded the Kanva dynasty, and how long did it last?

Vasudeva Kanva (73–64 BCE) — a minister of Devabhuti, the last Shunga ruler. The Kanva dynasty lasted about 45 years (73–28 BCE). It was overthrown by the Satavahanas.

15. Who founded the Satavahana dynasty, and what was their capital?

Simuka (c. 1st century BCE). Capital: Pratishthana (Paithan, Maharashtra). The Satavahanas are also called the Andhras.

16. Who was Gautamiputra Satakarni, and what was his title?

Gautamiputra Satakarni (106–130 CE) — the greatest Satavahana ruler. Title: Trisamudratoyapitavahana ("one whose horses drank the waters of the three oceans"). He defeated Nahapana, the Western Kshatrapa ruler. His mother Gautami Balashri's Nashik inscription is the main source for his reign.

17. Which Satavahana king issued ship-type coins?

Yajna Sri Satakarni (165–194 CE) — his coins depict a ship with a mast, the most direct numismatic evidence of Satavahana maritime trade.

18. Who were the Kushans, and who founded their Indian branch?

The Kushans were a Central Asian dynasty (a branch of the Yuezhi confederation). Their Indian branch was founded by Kujula Kadphises (30–80 CE). Their capital was Purushapura (Peshawar).

19. Who started the Saka era, and in which year?

Kanishka (127–150 CE) — the Saka era began in 78 CE. The Indian National Calendar (adopted 22 March 1957) uses the Saka era.

20. Which Buddhist council was held under Kanishka, and where?

The Fourth Buddhist Council, held at Kundalvana in Kashmir, under Kanishka's patronage. It was presided over by Vasumitra and Ashvaghosha. Buddhism formally split into Mahayana and Hinayana at this council.

21. Who was the first Kushan ruler to issue gold coins in India?

Vima Kadphises (95–127 CE) — the first large-scale gold coinage in India, a direct consequence of the Silk Road trade.

22. Who were the Navaratna of Chandragupta II's court?

Kalidasa, Dhanvantari, Kshapanaka, Amarasimha, Shanku, Vetalabhatta, Ghatakarpara, Varahamihira, and Vararuchi. Kalidasa is the most famous — he wrote Abhijnanashakuntalam, Meghaduta, and Raghuvamsha.

23. Who founded the Gupta dynasty, and who started the Gupta era?

Sri Gupta (c. 240–280 CE) founded the dynasty. Chandragupta I (319–335 CE) started the Gupta era (319–320 CE) and took the title Maharajadhiraja.

24. Which Gupta king is called the "Napoleon of India", and by whom?

Samudragupta (335–375 CE), called the "Napoleon of India" by the British historian V. A. Smith. His conquests are recorded in the Allahabad Pillar Inscription (Prayag Prashasti) by his court poet Harisena.

25. Who composed the Allahabad Pillar Inscription, and whose reign does it describe?

Harisena — the court poet of Samudragupta. It describes Samudragupta's conquests — 9 kings of Aryavarta, 12 kings of Dakshinapatha, the Atavika kingdoms, and the frontier kingdoms.

26. Who was Chandragupta II, and what is his most famous title?

Chandragupta II (375–415 CE) — title Vikramaditya. His court was the golden age of Sanskrit literature (Kalidasa and the Navaratna). The Mehrauli Iron Pillar is attributed to him. Faxian (Fa-Hien) visited India during his reign.

27. Who founded Nalanda University?

Kumaragupta I (415–455 CE) — title Mahendraditya. Nalanda became the greatest centre of Buddhist learning in the ancient world.

28. Who defeated the Hunas, and which inscription records it?

Skandagupta (455–467 CE) — the last great Gupta ruler. His Bhitari Pillar Inscription records his victories over the Pushyamitras and the Hunas.

29. Who was the last recognised Gupta ruler, and when did the dynasty end?

Vishnugupta (c. 540–550 CE) — the last recognised Gupta ruler. The dynasty ended around 550 CE, when the Maukhari dynasty of Kanauj and the Later Guptas of Magadha emerged as successor states.

30. Who was the Chinese pilgrim who visited India during Chandragupta II's reign?

Faxian (Fa-Hien), 399–414 CE. His Record of Buddhist Kingdoms describes Gupta administration as mild and liberal — no capital punishment, no harsh penal code.

31. Which Greek name refers to Chandragupta Maurya, and which to Bindusara?

Chandragupta Maurya = Sandrakottos. Bindusara = Amitrochates (from Amitraghata).

32. What are the two great eras in Indian chronology, and who started each?

Saka era (78 CE) — Kanishka. Gupta era (319–320 CE) — Chandragupta I. The Saka era is used by the Indian National Calendar.

33. Which Mauryan ruler dedicated caves to the Ajivika sect, and where?

Dasharatha (232–224 BCE) — the Nagarjuni Hills inscription near Barabar, Bihar. He dedicated three caves to the Ajivikas.

34. Which Mauryan ruler is called "Jaina Ashoka"?

Samprati (224–215 BCE) — he is said to have built thousands of Jain temples and sent missionaries to South India and Sri Lanka.

35. Where is the Mehrauli Iron Pillar, and to which king is it attributed?

Delhi — the rust-free iron pillar in the Qutb complex. It is usually attributed to Chandragupta II (Gupta). The pillar has not rusted in 1,600 years — a metallurgical marvel.

Closing tip: on the night before the exam, only read the Rapid Fire section. If you can answer 30 of 35 out loud without tapping, this page has done its job.
