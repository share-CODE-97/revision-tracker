1. Carnatic Wars — The Anglo-French Struggle

Three wars, 1744–1763. Decided who would dominate South India — the British or the French.

First Carnatic War

1746 – 1748
WhereCarnatic (Coromandel coast), chiefly St. Thome near Madras
BritishCommodore Curtis Bennett (navy); Anwar-ud-Din, Nawab of Carnatic (local ally)
FrenchMarquis Joseph-François Dupleix (Governor-General); Admiral La Bourdonnais (navy); Captain Paradise (field)
ResultInconclusive on land; French captured Madras (1746). Battle of St. Thome proved European troops could beat a much larger Indian army.
TreatyTreaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) — Madras returned to Britain; part of the wider War of Austrian Succession
Aix-la-Chapelle St. Thome

Second Carnatic War

1749 – 1754
WhereCarnatic and Hyderabad — succession disputes for the Nawab of Carnatic and the Nizam
BritishSupported Muhammad Ali (Carnatic) and Nasir Jang (Hyderabad)
FrenchDupleix; supported Chanda Sahib (Carnatic) and Muzaffar Jang (Hyderabad)
ResultBritish victory; French influence in Hyderabad and Carnatic sharply reduced. Robert Clive emerged as a key commander.
TreatyTreaty of Pondicherry (1754) — both companies renounced involvement in local disputes; Dupleix recalled
Treaty of Pondicherry British ascendancy

Third Carnatic War

1757 – 1763
WhereCarnatic, Bengal, and the wider Seven Years' War theatre
BritishSir Eyre Coote — key victory at Battle of Wandiwash (1760)
FrenchCount de Lally — defeated and captured at Wandiwash
ResultDecisive British victory. French political ambitions in India ended. Pondicherry and Chandernagore returned but could not be fortified or garrisoned.
TreatyTreaty of Paris (1763) — ended the Seven Years' War; French confined to trading posts only
Treaty of Paris 1763 Wandiwash 1760 French threat ended

2. Battle of Plassey & Its Aftermath

Battle of Plassey

1757
WherePalashi (Plassey), on the banks of the Hooghly, about 150 km north of Calcutta
BritishRobert Clive — ~3,000 troops (European + sepoy). Key ally: Mir Jafar (conspired against the Nawab)
OpposingSiraj-ud-Daulah, Nawab of Bengal — ~50,000 troops; supported by French artillery
ResultBritish victory in under 40 minutes — largely because Mir Jafar's large contingent refused to fight. Siraj fled and was killed. Mir Jafar was installed as puppet Nawab.
TreatyNo single formal treaty — Clive signed a secret pact with Mir Jafar before the battle; EIC gained huge financial concessions and the seeds of territorial control.
British Mir Jafar's betrayal Bengal under EIC influence

Battle of Bedara (Chinsurah / Hooghly)

1759
WhereChinsurah, near Hooghly, Bengal — Dutch outpost
BritishColonel Francis Forde; Charles Wilson; Mir Jafar (now allied with British)
OpposingDutch East India Company — Colonel Jean-Baptiste Roussel. Invited by Mir Jafar to expel the British.
ResultBritish victory — Dutch defeated and forced to withdraw from Bengal. Ended Dutch commercial rivalry in the region.
TreatyNo major formal treaty — Dutch agreed to peace after the defeat; they never again challenged British power in Bengal.
British Dutch expelled Seven Years' War spillover

Battle of Buxar

1764
WhereBuxar, on the Ganges in Bihar (near the Caramnasa river)
BritishMajor Hector Munro — ~7,000 troops (857 British, ~5,300 sepoys, ~918 cavalry)
OpposingCombined forces of Mir Qasim (ex-Nawab of Bengal), Shuja-ud-Daulah (Nawab of Awadh), and Shah Alam II (Mughal Emperor)
ResultDecisive British victory. The confederacy was shattered. This battle is considered more decisive than Plassey — it confirmed British supremacy over Bengal and Bihar and broke the power of Awadh.
TreatyTreaty of Allahabad (1765) — Shah Alam II granted the Diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa to the EIC. This made the Company the real ruler of eastern India.
Treaty of Allahabad 1765 Diwani rights British

3. Anglo-Mysore Wars — The Southern Struggle

Four wars, 1767–1799. The most determined Indian resistance to British expansion in the South.

First Anglo-Mysore War

1767 – 1769
WhereMysore, Carnatic, and Madras
BritishEIC, allied with the Nizam and the Marathas
OpposingHyder Ali — de-facto ruler of Mysore; skilled diplomat and commander
ResultHyder Ali won. He used diplomacy to turn the Nizam and Marathas neutral, then appeared before Madras, forcing a humiliating peace on the British.
TreatyTreaty of Madras (1769) — conquered territories restored to each side; mutual assistance against foreign attack
British setback Treaty of Madras Hyder Ali wins

Second Anglo-Mysore War

1780 – 1784
WhereCarnatic and Mysore — major engagements at Pollilur and Arcot
BritishSir Eyre Coote — won the Battle of Porto Novo (1781), a key turning point
OpposingHyder Ali (died 1782); succeeded by his son Tipu Sultan, who continued the war
ResultStalemate / mutual exhaustion. Neither side could achieve a decisive victory. Tipu fought the British to a standstill after his father's death.
TreatyTreaty of Mangalore (1784) — both sides restored conquered territories; a return to the status quo ante bellum
Treaty of Mangalore Stalemate Tipu's rise

Third Anglo-Mysore War

1790 – 1792
WhereMysore — culminating in the Siege of Seringapatam (1792)
BritishLord Cornwallis (Governor-General), commanding EIC forces; allied with the Nizam and the Marathas
OpposingTipu Sultan
ResultBritish victory. Tipu forced to cede half of Mysore to the British and their allies. This broke Mysore's power as a major independent state.
TreatyTreaty of Seringapatam (1792) — Tipu surrendered Malabar, Coorg, Dindigul, Baramahal, and parts of Berar; also paid a huge indemnity
Treaty of Seringapatam British Mysore weakened

Fourth Anglo-Mysore War

1799
WhereSeringapatam — the capital of Mysore
BritishLord Wellesley (Governor-General); General Harris commanded the assault; Major-General Baird led the storming party
OpposingTipu Sultan
ResultDecisive British victory. Seringapatam stormed on 4 May 1799; Tipu Sultan was killed in the fighting. Mysore partitioned; the Wodeyar dynasty restored under British control.
TreatyPartition Treaty of Mysore (1799) — EIC took the southern portion and Seringapatam; Nizam got a share. Mysore became a subsidiary state.
Tipu killed British Mysore partitioned

4. Anglo-Afghan Wars — The North-West Frontier

1839–1919. The "Great Game" — Britain vs Russia, with Afghanistan in between.

First Anglo-Afghan War

1839 – 1842
WhereAfghanistan — Kabul, and the disastrous retreat through the Khyber Pass
BritishLord Auckland (Governor-General); Sir William Macnaghten (envoy); General Elphinstone (commander)
OpposingAfghan tribes and Dost Mohammad Khan (initially); later his son Akbar Khan led resistance
ResultCatastrophic British defeat. The British installed Shah Shuja but faced mass uprising. In January 1842, the retreating army of ~16,000 was annihilated in the Khyber Pass — only one survivor (Dr. Brydon) reached Jalalabad. A "Army of Retribution" later reoccupied Kabul but the war ended in British withdrawal.
TreatyNo formal treaty — British withdrew; Dost Mohammad Khan returned to the throne. This war shattered the myth of British invincibility.
British disaster Khyber Pass retreat Myth broken

Second Anglo-Afghan War

1878 – 1880
WhereAfghanistan — Kabul, Kandahar, and the Khyber Pass region
BritishLord Lytton (Viceroy); General Roberts (later Lord Roberts) — famous march from Kabul to Kandahar
OpposingEmir Sher Ali Khan; after his death, his son Yaqub Khan; later Ayub Khan (Sher Ali's son) led resistance
ResultBritish military victory but strategic limited. British occupied Kabul and much of Afghanistan. The murder of British envoy Cavagnari in 1879 led to renewed fighting. General Roberts' march to Kandahar (1880) was a decisive victory. Britain gained control of Afghan foreign affairs but could not permanently occupy the country.
TreatyTreaty of Gandamak (1879) — Yaqub Khan agreed to conduct foreign relations per British advice; British got control of the Khyber Pass and other frontier areas. Later, Treaty of Rawalpindi (1919) ended the Third War and granted Afghanistan control of its foreign affairs.
Treaty of Gandamak 1879 British (limited) "Great Game"

4A. Anglo-Sikh Wars — The Punjab Campaign

1845–1849. The last major Indian power to fall to the British. Two short, brutal wars ended the Sikh Empire.

First Anglo-Sikh War

1845 – 1846
WherePunjab — Mudki, Ferozeshah, Aliwal, and Sobraon (near the Sutlej)
BritishSir Hugh Gough (Commander-in-Chief); Sir Henry Hardinge (Governor-General); Sir Harry Smith (Aliwal)
OpposingSikh Empire — Lal Singh (Vizier) and Tej Singh (Commander). Both were suspected of treason and failed to press advantages.
ResultBritish victory. Despite fierce Sikh resistance at Ferozeshah and Sobraon, the British won decisively. The Sikh army was defeated and the young Maharaja Duleep Singh was placed under British protection.
TreatyTreaty of Lahore (1846) — Sikhs ceded the Jullundur Doab and Kashmir (later sold to Gulab Singh). A British Resident was installed at Lahore. Treaty of Bhairowal (1846) — a British-controlled Regency Council was set up for Duleep Singh.
Treaty of Lahore 1846 Treaty of Bhairowal British Sobraon

Second Anglo-Sikh War

1848 – 1849
WherePunjab — Ramnagar, Chillianwala, and Gujrat
BritishSir Hugh Gough; Lord Dalhousie (Governor-General); Sir Charles Napier later took command
OpposingSher Singh Attariwala and Chattar Singh Attariwala — led a revolt against the British Resident
ResultBritish victory. The Battle of Chillianwala (1849) was a costly and confused British setback, but Gough won decisively at Gujrat (1849). The Sikh army surrendered, and the British annexed the Punjab.
TreatyNo treaty — Punjab was formally annexed by Lord Dalhousie on 29 March 1849. Maharaja Duleep Singh was pensioned off and sent to England.
British Punjab annexed 1849 Chillianwala Gujrat

5. Anglo-Burmese Wars — The Eastern Frontier

1824–1885. Three wars that ended the Konbaung dynasty and brought Burma into British India.

First Anglo-Burmese War

1824 – 1826
WhereBurma — Rangoon, Arakan, Assam, and Manipur
BritishLord Amherst (Governor-General); Sir Archibald Campbell (commander); Sir Edward Paget (naval)
OpposingKing Bagyidaw; General Maha Bandula (killed in action)
ResultBritish victory. Burma ceded Assam, Manipur, Arakan, and Tenasserim. The war was extremely costly for both sides — the British suffered massive losses from disease.
TreatyTreaty of Yandabo (1826) — Burma ceded the above territories and paid a large indemnity; British resident installed at Ava
Treaty of Yandabo British Assam, Arakan ceded

Second Anglo-Burmese War

1852
WhereLower Burma — Rangoon, Martaban, Bassein, Pegu, Prome
BritishLord Dalhousie (Governor-General); Commodore Lushington (naval)
OpposingKing Pagan Min — the Burmese governor of Rangoon had mistreated British subjects
ResultBritish victory. Lower Burma annexed. The war was brief and one-sided — Burmese forces were quickly driven out of key ports. This was the most blatantly commercial war of the three.
TreatyNo formal treaty — Dalhousie simply annexed the province of Pegu by proclamation. The war ended in December 1852.
British Lower Burma annexed Dalhousie

Third Anglo-Burmese War

1885
WhereUpper Burma — advance up the Irrawaddy River to Mandalay
BritishLord Dufferin (Viceroy); General Harry Prendergast (commander); General Garnet Wolseley
OpposingKing Thibaw Min — the last Konbaung king; the Burmese offered almost no resistance
ResultBritish victory in under a month. Mandalay occupied on 28 November 1885 without a battle. King Thibaw deposed and exiled to India. The entire country was annexed on 1 January 1886.
TreatyNo treaty — unconditional annexation. Upper Burma became a province of British India.
British Konbaung dynasty ends Thibaw exiled

6. Anglo-Nepal War (Gorkha War)

Anglo-Nepal War (Gorkha War)

1814 – 1816
WhereNepal — the Himalayan foothills and the Terai region
BritishLord Hastings (Governor-General); General David Ochterlony (key commander); General Rollo Gillespie (killed at Battle of Nalapani)
OpposingGorkhali army of Nepal — led by Amar Singh Thapa and other Thapa commanders
ResultBritish victory. The Gorkhas fought fiercely — the siege of Nalapani was one of the hardest battles. But Ochterlony's capture of the hill forts (especially Malaun) broke Nepalese resistance.
TreatyTreaty of Sugauli (1816) — Nepal renounced all claims to the Terai and ceded all conquests west of the Kali River up to the Sutlej. Britain gained strategic hill stations (including Shimla) and the Gorkha recruitment into the British Indian Army began.
Treaty of Sugauli 1816 British Gorkha recruitment

BPSC / Exam Focus — High-Yield Facts

Fact Why it matters
Plassey vs Buxar Plassey (1757) = conspiracy + Mir Jafar's betrayal. Buxar (1764) = real military victory. Buxar led to the Diwani of Bengal (Treaty of Allahabad, 1765) — the constitutional beginning of British rule in India.
Battle of Bedara (1759) Frequently asked as "Battle of Chinsurah" or "Battle of Hooghly." It ended Dutch ambitions in Bengal. Part of the Seven Years' War spillover.
Treaty of Allahabad (1765) Shah Alam II granted Diwani rights (Bengal, Bihar, Orissa) to the EIC. This is the single most important treaty for the "dual government" phase in Bengal.
1st Mysore War — Treaty of Madras The only war Hyder Ali won against the British. Often asked as "Who defeated the British in the First Anglo-Mysore War?"
4th Mysore War — Tipu's death Tipu Sultan died on 4 May 1799 defending Seringapatam. The Wodeyar dynasty was restored under British control. Mysore became a subsidiary state.
Treaty of Seringapatam (1792) Tipu ceded half his kingdom — Malabar, Coorg, Dindigul, Baramahal. This is a classic MCQ on the Third Mysore War.
1st Afghan War — Khyber Pass The retreat of 1842 is one of the most famous disasters in British military history. Only Dr. Brydon survived to reach Jalalabad. Frequently asked in BPSC.
Treaty of Yandabo (1826) Ended the 1st Burma War. Burma ceded Assam, Manipur, Arakan, and Tenasserim. This is how Assam became part of British India.
Treaty of Sugauli (1816) Ended the Anglo-Nepal War. Nepal ceded the Terai and territories west of the Kali River. Britain gained Shimla and hill stations. The Gorkha recruitment into the British Army began after this treaty.
Battle of Nalapani (1814) Part of the Anglo-Nepal War. General Rollo Gillespie was killed. The Gorkhas fought so fiercely that the British later erected a monument to their bravery.
Chain mnemonic for the "Anglo Wars": C → P → B → M → A → B → N — Carnatic → Plassey → Buxar → Mysore → Afghan → Burma → Nepal.
Three "decisive" battles: Plassey (political), Buxar (constitutional), Wandiwash (French eliminated).
Three "disaster / setback" moments: 1st Afghan War (Kabul retreat), 1st Mysore War (Treaty of Madras), and the early phase of the Anglo-Nepal War (Nalapani).
Three wars where a treaty created lasting territorial change: Allahabad (1765) → Diwani; Seringapatam (1792) → Mysore reduced; Sugauli (1816) → Nepal cedes Terai.
