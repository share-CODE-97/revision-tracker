President of India — Powers, Election & Articles

The constitutional head of the Republic — Articles 52 to 78 of Part V. The most-asked Polity topic in Prelims, and the one where article numbers decide the answer.

How to use this page: every card is closed by default. Tap it — or hit Reveal all — only after you have tried to recall the article number, the power, and the limitation. Reading an open card teaches you nothing. Guessing first is what makes it stick.

1 · The Constitutional Articles — the master table

Part V, Chapter I (Articles 52–78) deals with the Union Executive. These are the articles you must know by number.

Article 52 — The President of India Art 52
Text: "There shall be a President of India." What it does: Establishes the office of the President as the constitutional head of the Republic. This is the foundational article — every other article about the President flows from this one. Exam keyword: "Which article establishes the office of the President?" — Article 52.
Article 53 — Executive Power of the Union Art 53 V.Imp
Text: "The executive power of the Union shall be vested in the President and shall be exercised by him either directly or through officers subordinate to him in accordance with this Constitution." Key sub-clauses:
  • Article 53(2): The supreme command of the Defence Forces of the Union is vested in the President.
  • Article 53(3): This article does not transfer to the President any functions conferred by law on state governments or other authorities.
Exam trap: The President is the supreme commander of the armed forces, but the exercise of this command is regulated by law — not by the President's personal discretion.
Article 54 — Election of President Art 54 V.Imp
Electoral College consists of:
  • (a) The elected members of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha).
  • (b) The elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States.
Explanation: "State" includes the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union Territory of Puducherry. (Added by the 70th Amendment Act, 1992.) Who is NOT in the electoral college:
  • Nominated members of Parliament (both Houses)
  • Nominated members of State Legislative Assemblies
  • Members of State Legislative Councils (MLCs)
Exam keyword: "Who elects the President?" — Only elected members of Parliament and elected members of State Legislative Assemblies. Nominated members are excluded.
Article 55 — Manner of Election Art 55 V.Imp
Three principles:
  • Uniformity in the scale of representation of different States inter se.
  • Parity between the States as a whole and the Union.
  • Proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote system.
Vote value formula (the part Prelims tests): MLA vote value = (Population of State ÷ Total elected MLAs of State) ÷ 1000 MP vote value = (Total value of all MLA votes) ÷ (Total elected MPs of both Houses) Population basis: The 1971 census is used until the first census after 2026 is published. (This was extended by the 84th Amendment Act, 2001.) Exam trap: The election is indirect — no citizen votes directly. It is also proportional (not first-past-the-post) and the voting is secret.
Article 56 — Term of Office Art 56
Term: 5 years from the date of entering office. The three provisos:
  • (a) The President may resign by writing to the Vice-President.
  • (b) The President may be removed by impeachment for violation of the Constitution (Article 61).
  • (c) The President shall continue in office after the expiry of the term until the successor enters office.
Article 57 — Eligibility for Re-election Art 57
Text: "A person who holds, or who has held, office as President shall, subject to the other provisions of this Constitution, be eligible for re-election to that office." Significance: There is no limit on the number of times a person can be re-elected as President. (In contrast, the US President is limited to two terms by the 22nd Amendment.) Exam keyword: "How many times can a person be re-elected as President of India?" — Any number of times. The Constitution imposes no limit.
Article 58 — Qualifications Art 58 V.Imp
To be eligible for election as President, a person must:
  • (a) Be a citizen of India.
  • (b) Have completed the age of 35 years.
  • (c) Be qualified for election as a member of the House of the People (Lok Sabha).
  • (d) Not hold any office of profit under the Government of India, any State, or any local authority.
Exceptions (Article 58(2)): A person shall not be deemed to hold an office of profit merely because they are:
  • President or Vice-President
  • Governor of a State
  • Minister of the Union or any State
Article 59 — Conditions of Office Art 59
The President shall not:
  • Be a member of either House of Parliament or of a State Legislature. If elected, they are deemed to have vacated that seat.
  • Hold any other office of profit.
Emoluments: The President is entitled to emoluments, allowances, and privileges as determined by Parliament. These cannot be diminished during the President's term.
Article 60 — Oath or Affirmation Art 60
Administered by: The Chief Justice of India (or in their absence, the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court available). The oath: The President swears to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law" and to "devote myself to the service and well-being of the people of India." Exam keyword: "Who administers the oath to the President?" — The Chief Justice of India. Not the Vice-President, not the Speaker.
Article 61 — Impeachment Art 61 V.Imp
Ground: "Violation of the Constitution" — the only ground. Note the phrase: it is broad and undefined, deliberately so. Procedure:
  • Step 1: The charge may be preferred by either House of Parliament.
  • Step 2: A resolution must be moved after at least 14 days' notice in writing, signed by not less than one-fourth of the total members of that House.
  • Step 3: The resolution must be passed by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the total membership of that House.
  • Step 4: The other House investigates the charge. The President has the right to appear and be represented.
  • Step 5: If the investigating House also passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority of its total membership declaring the charge sustained, the President is removed from office.
Key facts:
  • Nominated members of Parliament can participate in impeachment proceedings (unlike in the election of the President).
  • Elected members of State Legislative Assemblies do not participate — impeachment is a parliamentary process only.
  • No President has ever been impeached in India.
Article 62 — Time of Holding Election Art 62
Rule: An election to fill a vacancy caused by the expiration of the term must be completed before the expiration of the term. Casual vacancy (death, resignation, removal): The election must be held within 6 months of the vacancy.
Article 71 — Disputes Relating to Election Art 71
Text: All doubts and disputes arising out of or in connection with the election of the President or Vice-President shall be inquired into and decided by the Supreme Court, whose decision shall be final. Key point: If the election of a President is declared void by the Supreme Court, acts done by them before the date of the decision are not invalidated. Exam keyword: "Who decides disputes relating to the election of the President?" — The Supreme Court of India (not the Election Commission).
Article 72 — Pardoning Power Art 72 V.Imp
The President can grant: Pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, or commute a sentence — or suspend, remit, or commute a sentence. Applicable in these cases (the jurisdictional test):
  • (a) All cases where the punishment or sentence is by a Court Martial.
  • (b) All cases where the punishment is for an offence against a law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the Union extends.
  • (c) All cases where the sentence is a sentence of death.
Limitation: The President's pardoning power does not extend to sentences by a court martial where the law itself confers the power on an officer of the armed forces (Article 72(2)). Exam trap: The President has pardoning power in all death sentence cases. The Governor does not — the Governor's power under Article 161 does not extend to death sentences.
Article 74 — Council of Ministers to Aid and Advise Art 74 V.Imp
Text: "There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President, who shall in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice." The 42nd Amendment (1976): Made the President bound by ministerial advice. The 44th Amendment (1978): Added a proviso — the President may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice, but must act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration. This is the President's only substantive check. Exam keyword: Article 74(1) — the President acts on ministerial advice. The 44th Amendment gave the President the right to return advice once, but not to reject it.
Article 75 — Appointment of Ministers Art 75
The President appoints: The Prime Minister, and on the PM's advice, the other Ministers. The PM's appointment: The President invites the leader of the party with a majority in the Lok Sabha to be PM. In a hung Parliament, the President exercises discretion in inviting the person most likely to command a majority. Tenure: Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President — but in practice, the PM's advice determines this. Collective responsibility: Article 75(3) — the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.
Article 78 — PM's Duty to Furnish Information Art 78
The Prime Minister must:
  • Communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation.
  • Furnish such information relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation as the President may call for.
  • If the President requires, submit for the consideration of the Council of Ministers any matter on which a decision has been taken by a Minister but which has not been considered by the Council.
Exam keyword: Article 78 is the President's information channel — the PM must keep the President informed. This is the basis of the President's right to be consulted.
Article 123 — Ordinance Making Power Art 123 V.Imp
Power: The President can promulgate ordinances when both Houses of Parliament are not in session (or when either House is not in session in the case of a unicameral legislature — not applicable at the Union level). Conditions:
  • The President must be satisfied that immediate action is necessary.
  • The ordinance must be laid before both Houses of Parliament within 6 weeks of the reassembly of Parliament.
  • If it is not approved within that period, it ceases to operate.
Judicial review: The satisfaction of the President is not beyond judicial review — the 44th Amendment made the ordinance-making power justiciable on grounds of malafide or abuse of power. The Supreme Court in DC Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1986) held that repeated re-promulgation of ordinances is a fraud on the Constitution. Exam trap: The ordinance power is co-extensive with Parliament's legislative power — it cannot be used to amend the Constitution.

2 · The Election — How the President is actually chosen

Indirect election, proportional representation, single transferable vote. The method is as important as the outcome.

V.V.Imp — The vote value calculation: This is the single most-asked numerical question on the President's election.
MLA vote value = (Population of State ÷ Total elected MLAs) ÷ 1000
MP vote value = (Total value of all MLA votes) ÷ (Total elected MPs)
The 1971 census is the basis until the first census after 2026.
Example: If a state has a population of 5 crore and 200 MLAs, MLA vote value = (5,00,00,000 ÷ 200) ÷ 1000 = 2,50,000 ÷ 1000 = 250.
Who votes — and who does not Art 54
Voters: Elected members of Lok Sabha, elected members of Rajya Sabha, elected members of State Legislative Assemblies (including Delhi and Puducherry). Non-voters:
  • Nominated members of Parliament (both Houses)
  • Nominated members of State Assemblies
  • Members of State Legislative Councils (MLCs)
  • Elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of Union Territories without legislatures (e.g., Ladakh, Lakshadweep)
Proportional Representation & Single Transferable Vote Art 55(3)
What it means: Voters rank candidates in order of preference. If no candidate gets the required quota on the first count, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated, and their votes are transferred to the next preference on those ballots. Quota: The winning candidate must secure more than 50% of the total valid votes (the "quota"). Why this system: It ensures the President is elected by a broad consensus, not by a simple plurality. It also gives representation to smaller parties and independents.

3 · The Powers — five categories, one office

Executive, Legislative, Financial, Judicial, and Emergency. Every Prelims question on the President falls into one of these five buckets.

Executive Powers Executive
Administrative head: All executive actions of the Government of India are formally taken in the President's name (Article 77). Appointments: The President appoints the PM, other Ministers, the Attorney General, the CAG, the CEC and Election Commissioners, the Chairman and members of UPSC, Governors of States, and members of the Finance Commission. Supreme Commander: The supreme command of the Defence Forces is vested in the President (Article 53(2)). Administration of UTs: The President administers Union Territories through administrators appointed by him. Scheduled Areas: The President can declare any area as a Scheduled Area and has powers over its administration. Information: The President can seek any information from the PM relating to the administration of the Union (Article 78).
Legislative Powers Legislative
Part of Parliament: The President is an integral part of Parliament (Article 79) — no bill becomes law without presidential assent. Summoning & Prorogation: The President summons and prorogues both Houses of Parliament, and can dissolve the Lok Sabha (Article 85). Special Address: The President addresses both Houses assembled together at the commencement of the first session after each general election and at the first session of each year (Article 87). Nomination: The President nominates 12 members to the Rajya Sabha (persons with special knowledge in literature, science, art, and social service) and 2 Anglo-Indian members to the Lok Sabha (Article 331 — this provision expired in 2020). Ordinance: The President can promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session (Article 123). Prior recommendation: Certain bills (e.g., Money Bills, bills involving expenditure from the Consolidated Fund) can be introduced only with the President's prior recommendation.
Financial Powers Financial
Money Bills: No Money Bill can be introduced in Parliament without the President's recommendation. Annual Budget: The President causes the Annual Financial Statement (Budget) to be laid before Parliament. Contingency Fund: The President can make advances from the Contingency Fund of India to meet unforeseen expenditure. Finance Commission: The President constitutes the Finance Commission every fifth year (Article 280). Emergency: The President can declare a Financial Emergency under Article 360.
Judicial Powers Judicial
Appointments: The President appoints the Chief Justice of India and other judges of the Supreme Court, and the Chief Justice and judges of High Courts. Pardoning Power (Article 72): Pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, commute — in all cases of Court Martial, Union law offences, and death sentences. Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143): The President can refer a question of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its advisory opinion. The opinion is not binding on the President. Disputes (Article 71): Disputes relating to the election of the President and Vice-President are decided by the Supreme Court.
Emergency Powers Emergency V.Imp
Three types of emergency:
  • National Emergency (Article 352): On grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The President can proclaim this even before the actual occurrence if there is imminent danger.
  • President's Rule (Article 356): On failure of constitutional machinery in a State. Based on the Governor's report or otherwise.
  • Financial Emergency (Article 360): If the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. Never declared so far.
Parliamentary approval: A National Emergency must be approved by both Houses within one month (44th Amendment; earlier two months). President's Rule must be approved within two months. Financial Emergency must be approved within two months. Judicial review: The 44th Amendment made the proclamation of National Emergency subject to judicial review on grounds of malafide. The S.R. Bommai case (1994) made Article 356 proclamations subject to judicial review.

4 · The Veto Powers — Absolute, Suspensive, Pocket

When the President refuses assent. The Indian President has three vetoes — but no "qualified veto".

Absolute Veto Veto
What it is: The President withholds assent to a bill passed by Parliament, and the bill ends there. It does not become law. When used: In the case of private members' bills, or bills passed by a previous government that has since lost its majority. Example: In 1954, President Rajendra Prasad used the absolute veto on the PEPSU Appropriation Bill. In 1991, President R. Venkataraman used it on the Salary, Allowances and Pension of Members of Parliament (Amendment) Bill.
Suspensive Veto Veto
What it is: The President returns a non-Money Bill to Parliament for reconsideration. If Parliament passes the bill again (with or without amendments) and sends it back, the President must give assent. Applicable to: Non-Money Bills only. The President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration. Example: In 2006, President APJ Abdul Kalam returned the Office of Profit Bill for reconsideration.
Pocket Veto Veto
What it is: The President simply keeps the bill pending indefinitely — neither assents, nor rejects, nor returns it. The Indian Constitution prescribes no time limit for the President to act on a bill. Example: In 1986, President Giani Zail Singh used the pocket veto on the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill — he neither assented nor returned it, and the bill lapsed. Exam trap: The pocket veto is a consequence of the Constitution's silence on a time limit, not an explicitly granted power. It is a discretionary power.
V.V.Imp — The three vetoes in one line: Absolute = the bill dies. Suspensive = the bill goes back once, but Parliament can override. Pocket = the bill is kept in limbo, with no time limit. India has no qualified veto (unlike the US President, who can veto and require a two-thirds majority to override).

5 · Discretionary Powers — when the President acts alone

Article 74 says the President acts on ministerial advice. But there are situations where the President exercises genuine discretion.

Discretion in Appointing the Prime Minister V.Imp
Situation: A hung Parliament — no party or coalition has a clear majority in the Lok Sabha. President's discretion: Invite the leader most likely to command a majority in the House. The President may also ask the person to prove their majority within a specified time. Example: In 1996, President Shankar Dayal Sharma invited Atal Bihari Vajpayee to form the government despite the BJP not having a majority, because it was the single-largest party. In 1998, the same situation led to a similar decision.
Discretion in Dissolving the Lok Sabha V.Imp
Situation: The Council of Ministers recommends dissolution of the Lok Sabha after a defeat or loss of majority. President's discretion: The President may refuse dissolution if there is a viable alternative government, or if the recommendation appears malafide. Judicial guidance: In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court held that the President's satisfaction in dissolving the Lok Sabha is subject to judicial review.
Discretion in Dismissing the Council of Ministers V.Imp
Situation: The Council of Ministers loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha but refuses to resign. President's discretion: The President can dismiss the Council of Ministers. This is an extreme step and has never been used at the Union level in India. Note: The President cannot dismiss a Council of Ministers that enjoys the confidence of the House — the President is not a political arbiter.
Discretion in Assent to Bills Veto
Situation: A bill passed by Parliament is presented for assent. President's discretion: The President may withhold assent, return the bill (if non-Money), or use the pocket veto. But if Parliament passes the bill again, assent is mandatory. Note: The President cannot withhold assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill — the 24th Amendment (1971) made assent mandatory for such bills.
Discretion in Referring Matters to the Supreme Court Art 143
Power: The President can refer a question of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its advisory opinion. Nature: The opinion is not binding on the President. The Supreme Court may also decline to answer. Example: In 1993, President Shankar Dayal Sharma referred the Ayodhya dispute to the Supreme Court. In 2004, the Gujarat Assembly dissolution issue was referred.
Judicial pronouncements on discretionary powers V.Imp
Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974): The Supreme Court held that the President and Governors must act on ministerial advice except in exceptional cases where the Constitution expressly requires them to act in their discretion. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Limited the misuse of Article 356 — made President's Rule subject to judicial review. Rameshwar Prasad Case (2006): Struck down the dissolution of the Bihar Assembly (2005), ruling it unconstitutional. Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016): Held that the Governor (representing the President) cannot interfere arbitrarily in legislative matters.

7 · Memory tricks that save you

The articles in order — by function. "52 Exists, 53 Executes, 54 Elects, 55 Method, 56 Term, 57 Re-elect, 58 Qualify, 59 Conditions, 60 Oath, 61 Impeach"
Read it as a story: 52 says there shall be a President → 53 gives them executive power → 54–55 explain how they are elected → 56–57 say how long they serve and whether they can return → 58–59 list what they must be and must not do → 60 is the oath → 61 is the exit door.
The three vetoes — in one line each. "Absolute kills, Suspensive returns, Pocket waits"
Absolute — the bill dies. Suspensive — the bill goes back once, but Parliament can override. Pocket — the bill is kept pending indefinitely. India has no qualified veto. If a question asks "which veto does the Indian President NOT have?" — the answer is qualified veto.
The three emergencies — by article and approval time. "352 National, 356 President's Rule, 360 Financial"
352 — National Emergency; approval within 1 month (44th Amendment; earlier 2 months). 356 — President's Rule; approval within 2 months. 360 — Financial Emergency; approval within 2 months. Three articles, three numbers — never mix them.
The pardoning power — the three jurisdictional tests. "Court Martial, Union Law, Death Sentence"
Article 72 gives the President pardoning power in exactly three categories: (a) Court Martial sentences, (b) offences against Union laws, (c) death sentences. The Governor's power (Article 161) covers state laws and does not extend to death sentences. This is a permanent Prelims trap.
The 42nd vs 44th Amendment — the Article 74 story. "42 bound, 44 bound but can return once"
42nd Amendment (1976): Made the President bound by ministerial advice — no discretion. 44th Amendment (1978): Kept the binding nature, but gave the President the right to return advice once for reconsideration. After reconsideration, the President must accept the advice. The 44th Amendment is the source of the President's only real check on the Council of Ministers.
The electoral college — who is in, who is out. "Elected in, Nominated out, MLCs never"
In: Elected MPs + Elected MLAs (including Delhi and Puducherry). Out: Nominated MPs, Nominated MLAs, all MLCs. If a question asks "who participates in the election of the President?", the answer is only elected legislators. Nominated members are deliberately excluded to keep the process democratic.
The vote value formula — in plain English. "MLA = (Population ÷ MLAs) ÷ 1000"
"MP = (Total MLA votes) ÷ (Total elected MPs)"
The 1971 census is used until the first census after 2026. The formula ensures uniformity across states and parity between the Union and the States. This is the most numerical part of the topic — practice with a sample calculation.
Four traps to watch. ① The President is not elected directly by the people — it is an indirect election. ② The President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration — only non-Money Bills. ③ The President cannot withhold assent to a Constitutional Amendment Bill — assent is mandatory. ④ Nominated members of Parliament can vote in impeachment but cannot vote in the election of the President. This asymmetry is a favourite Prelims question.

8 · Rapid Fire

Cover the whole page. Answer out loud before you tap.

1. Which article establishes the office of the President of India?

Article 52 — "There shall be a President of India."

2. Which article vests the executive power of the Union in the President?

Article 53(1). Article 53(2) vests the supreme command of the Defence Forces in the President.

3. Who elects the President of India — and who is excluded from the electoral college?

Elected members of both Houses of Parliament and elected members of State Legislative Assemblies (including Delhi and Puducherry). Excluded: nominated members of Parliament, nominated members of State Assemblies, and all MLCs.

4. What is the vote value formula for an MLA in the presidential election?

MLA vote value = (Population of the State ÷ Total elected MLAs of the State) ÷ 1000. The 1971 census is used until the first census after 2026.

5. What is the term of office of the President, and can they be re-elected?

5 years (Article 56). Article 57 allows re-election any number of times — there is no limit.

6. What are the qualifications to be elected as President?

Article 58: (a) Citizen of India, (b) Completed 35 years of age, (c) Qualified for election as a member of the Lok Sabha, (d) Not holding any office of profit. Article 58(2) exempts the President, Vice-President, Governor, and Ministers from the "office of profit" disqualification.

7. Who administers the oath to the President of India?

The Chief Justice of India (Article 60) — or in their absence, the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court available.

8. On what ground can the President be impeached, and under which article?

Article 61 — on the ground of "violation of the Constitution."

9. What is the procedure for impeachment of the President?

The charge can be preferred by either House. A resolution must be moved after 14 days' notice signed by at least 1/4th of the members of that House, and passed by a two-thirds majority of the total membership of that House. The other House investigates. If it also passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority of its total membership sustaining the charge, the President is removed.

10. Who participates in the impeachment of the President — and who does not?

All MPs (elected + nominated) participate. State MLAs do not participate.

11. Who decides disputes relating to the election of the President?

The Supreme Court of India (Article 71). Its decision is final.

12. What is the pardoning power of the President, and under which article?

Article 72 — pardons, reprieves, respites, remission, or commutation of sentences. Applicable in: (a) Court Martial cases, (b) offences against Union laws, (c) all death sentence cases.

13. Can the President pardon a death sentence? Can the Governor?

The President can (Article 72). The Governor cannot — Article 161 does not extend to death sentences.

14. What does Article 74 say, and how was it amended?

Article 74 says the President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. The 42nd Amendment (1976) made this binding. The 44th Amendment (1978) added the proviso that the President may require reconsideration of advice once, but must act on the advice tendered after such reconsideration.

15. What is the ordinance-making power of the President, and under which article?

Article 123 — the President can promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session. The ordinance must be laid before Parliament within 6 weeks of its reassembly and ceases to operate if not approved.

16. What are the three types of veto available to the Indian President?

Absolute veto, suspensive veto, and pocket veto. India does not have a qualified veto.

17. What is a pocket veto, and who used it in India?

The President keeps a bill pending indefinitely — neither assenting, rejecting, nor returning it. President Giani Zail Singh used it in 1986 on the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill.

18. Can the President return a Money Bill for reconsideration?

No. The suspensive veto (returning a bill) applies only to non-Money Bills. The President cannot return a Money Bill.

19. What are the three types of emergency the President can declare?

National Emergency (Article 352), President's Rule (Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360).

20. What is the approval time for a National Emergency?

Within one month by both Houses of Parliament (44th Amendment; earlier it was two months).

21. What is the advisory jurisdiction of the President?

Article 143 — the President can refer a question of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its advisory opinion. The opinion is not binding on the President.

22. Who nominates members to the Rajya Sabha, and how many?

The President nominates 12 members to the Rajya Sabha (Article 80) — persons with special knowledge in literature, science, art, and social service.

23. What is the President's role in the appointment of the Prime Minister in a hung Parliament?

The President exercises discretion in inviting the leader most likely to command a majority in the Lok Sabha. The President may ask the person to prove their majority within a specified time.

24. What did the S.R. Bommai case (1994) decide about the President's powers?

It limited the misuse of Article 356 (President's Rule) by making the proclamation subject to judicial review. It also held that the President's satisfaction in dissolving the Lok Sabha is subject to judicial review.

Closing tip: on the night before the exam, only read the Rapid Fire section. If you can answer 20 of 24 out loud without tapping, this page has done its job.
